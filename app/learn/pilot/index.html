Pilot / feasibility study

Can the full study be done, and how?

A small rehearsal of a future study. Can we recruit, retain, deliver and measure? Not for estimating effects.

How it works

  1. Ask about feasibility

    Name what is uncertain about the future main study: recruitment, retention, delivery, measurement. Those are the pilot's questions.

    “a feasibility study for a future definitive RCT asks whether the future trial can be done, should be done, and, if so, how.”

    Eldridge et al. (2016), introduction

    “The rationale of a pilot trial is to investigate areas of uncertainty about the future definitive RCT”

    Eldridge et al. (2016), box 1

  2. Set criteria first

    Before starting, write down what result means go, go with changes, or stop.

    “This might involve formal progression criteria to decide whether to proceed, to proceed with amendments, or not to proceed”

    Eldridge et al. (2016), box 1

  3. Size for feasibility

    Choose the number of participants to answer the feasibility questions, and say why.

    “The number of participants in a pilot study should be based on the feasibility objectives and some rationale should be given”

    Eldridge et al. (2016), box 1

  4. Rehearse small

    Run the main study, or part of it, on a small scale, and count everyone at every stage.

    “in a pilot study (that might or might not be randomised) the future definitive RCT, or part of it, is conducted on a smaller scale.”

    Eldridge et al. (2016), introduction

    “the numbers of participants who were approached and/or assessed for eligibility, randomly assigned, received intended treatment, and were assessed for each objective”

    Eldridge et al. (2016), item 13a

  5. Decide, don't test

    Judge the counts against the criteria and decide on the main study. Do not test whether the treatment works: the pilot is too small for that.

    “Formal hypothesis testing for effectiveness (or efficacy) is not recommended.”

    Eldridge et al. (2016), box 1

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Low power

    Too small to see the effect, and too noisy to trust a hit.

    Work out how many you need before you start.

    “The aim of a pilot trial is not to assess effectiveness (or efficacy) and it will usually be underpowered to do this”

    Eldridge et al. (2016), box 1

  • Limited generalizability

    True here, but maybe not anywhere else.

    Run it where the results will be used.
