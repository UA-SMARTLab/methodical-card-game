Analytical observational / Method

Case-cohort / nested case–control

How can an expensive measurement be studied in a large cohort without measuring everyone?

Inside a cohort with stored samples, measure an expensive exposure only in all cases plus a random subcohort (or matched controls).

How it works

  1. Start with a cohort

    Take a large cohort that is followed up and has stored samples, where measuring everyone (a genotype, a biomarker) would cost too much.

    “Cohort sampling designs are used in follow-up studies when large cohorts are needed to observe enough cases but it is not feasible to collect data on all covariates for the whole cohort.”

    Kulathinal et al. (2007), section 1, Introduction

  2. Random subcohort

    Draw a random sample of the whole cohort, without looking at who later becomes a case.

    “a random subsample of the original cohort (subcohort), selected independently of the definition of cases”

    Kulathinal et al. (2007), section 1, Introduction

  3. Add every case

    Add every person who develops the outcome during follow-up. Measure the costly exposure only in this case-cohort set.

    “all cases outside the subcohort, i.e. all members of the cohort developing any or all events of interest during the follow-up. The union of (1) and (2) is referred to as the case-cohort set.”

    Kulathinal et al. (2007), section 1, Introduction

    “Commonly used designs such as the case-control or the nested case-control design require genotyping of all the cases and matched controls for each case.”

    Kulathinal et al. (2007), section 1, Introduction (the nested case–control alternative)

  4. Reuse for outcomes

    Because the subcohort was drawn at random, the same subcohort serves as comparison for several outcomes, and describes the whole cohort.

    “a distinct advantage of the case-cohort design is that the selected subcohort can be used for analysing several endpoints of interest.”

    Kulathinal et al. (2007), section 1, Introduction

  5. Weight the analysis

    Cases are over-represented, so the analysis weights people back to the whole cohort, for example by the inverse of the subcohort sampling fraction.

    “Adjustments to the partial likelihood are required because the cases are overrepresented in the case-cohort set and therefore unadjusted risk sets in the partial likelihood would not represent the original study cohort.”

    Kulathinal et al. (2007), section 1, Introduction

    “Here the subcohort members are weighted by the inverse of the sampling fraction at the event time”

    Kulathinal et al. (2007), section 6.2, Analysis of time-to-event data (Barlow's weighting)

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Measurement error

    The instrument is noisy, biased or drifting.

    Use instruments proven to measure what matters.

  • Selection bias

    Who ends up in the sample depends on what you're studying.

    Draw the causal map; adjust only for what belongs.

A real example

EPIC-InterAct: a random subcohort of about 16,000 drawn from 340,000 Europeans to study type 2 diabetes.

Defining reference: Prentice (1986). A case-cohort design for epidemiologic cohort studies and disease prevention trials. Biometrika.
