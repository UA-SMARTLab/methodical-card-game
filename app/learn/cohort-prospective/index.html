Analytical observational / Method

Prospective cohort

Do people with the exposure go on to get the outcome more often?

Measure exposures in people before outcomes happen, then follow them forward. Shows temporal order and incidence.

How it works

  1. Recruit

    Define who is eligible and enrol a group that is free of the outcome at the start.

    “Closed cohorts (for example birth cohorts) enrol a defined number of participants at study onset and follow them from that time forward, often at set intervals up to a fixed end date.”

    Vandenbroucke et al. (2007), box 1

  2. Measure exposure

    Record each person's exposure at the start, before any outcome has happened, so the time order is clear.

    “They obtain information about people and their exposures at baseline, let time pass, and then assess the occurrence of outcomes.”

    Vandenbroucke et al. (2007), box 1

  3. Follow up

    Follow everyone the same way for years, and keep track of who is lost along the way.

    “Knowing details about follow-up procedures, including whether procedures minimized non-response and loss to follow-up and whether the procedures were similar for all participants, informs judgments about the validity of results.”

    Vandenbroucke et al. (2007), item 6(a), explanation

  4. Count new cases

    Count the people who develop the outcome during follow-up, in each exposure group.

    “They may examine the number of people who have a disease at a particular time (prevalence), or that develop a disease over a defined period (incidence).”

    Vandenbroucke et al. (2007), box 7

  5. Compare risks

    Divide the risk (or rate) in the exposed by that in the unexposed. Adjust for confounders, because exposure was not assigned by chance.

    “In a closed cohort cumulative incidences (risks) and incidence rates can be estimated; when exposed and unexposed groups are compared, this leads to risk ratio or rate ratio estimates.”

    Vandenbroucke et al. (2007), box 1

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Confounding

    A hidden third factor drives both exposure and outcome.

    Draw the causal map; adjust only for what belongs.

    “In reality, the seeming association or lack of association is due to another factor that determines the occurrence of the disease but that is also associated with the exposure.”

    Vandenbroucke et al. (2007), box 5

  • Attrition & missing data

    The people who drop out are not like the people who stay.

    Analyse everyone as randomized, whatever happened next.

    “In other studies where follow-up procedures differed between exposed and unexposed groups, readers might recognize substantial bias due to unequal ascertainment of events or differences in non-response or loss to follow-up”

    Vandenbroucke et al. (2007), item 6(a), explanation

  • Reverse causation

    The outcome is causing the 'cause'.

    Leave a gap between exposure and outcome.

A real example

The Framingham Heart Study (1948–), which gave us the very idea of 'risk factors'.

Defining reference: Dawber et al. (1951). Epidemiological Approaches to Heart Disease: The Framingham Study. American Journal of Public Health and the Nations Health.
