Descriptive observational / Method

Monitoring / surveillance

Is it changing, where, and is something unusual happening?

Systematic, repeated measurement of the same quantity across time and place to detect trends and signals.

How it works

  1. Define a case

    Write down exactly what counts as a case: symptoms, test results, place and time, and how certain each level is.

    “A public health surveillance system is dependent on a clear case definition for the health-related event under surveillance”

    CDC (2001), Task B, describing the system

  2. Collect, always

    Gather the same data the same way, week after week and place by place, for as long as the system runs.

    “Public health surveillance is the ongoing, systematic collection, analysis, interpretation, and dissemination of data regarding a health-related event for use in public health action to reduce morbidity and mortality and to improve health”

    CDC (2001), Background

  3. Report fast

    Data flow from the people who see cases to those who can act. Every step between them takes time, and the delays can be measured.

    “Timeliness reflects the speed between steps in a public health surveillance system.”

    CDC (2001), D.2.h, Timeliness

  4. Spot the signal

    Compare the latest counts with the usual level for that place and season. A rise beyond it is a signal to investigate, not yet a cause.

    “detect trends that signal changes in the occurrence of disease, injury, or adverse or protective exposure, including detection of epidemics (or outbreaks)”

    CDC (2001), D.1, level of usefulness

  5. Know what's missed

    No system catches every case. A trend can still be trusted if the share caught stays the same; a change in reporting can look like a change in disease.

    “A public health surveillance system that does not have high sensitivity can still be useful in monitoring trends as long as the sensitivity remains reasonably constant over time.”

    CDC (2001), D.2.e, Sensitivity

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Measurement error

    The instrument is noisy, biased or drifting.

    Use instruments proven to measure what matters.

  • Selection bias

    Who ends up in the sample depends on what you're studying.

    Draw the causal map; adjust only for what belongs.

    “A public health surveillance system that is representative accurately describes the occurrence of a health-related event over time and its distribution in the population by place and person.”

    CDC (2001), D.2.g, Representativeness

A real example

The Mauna Loa CO₂ record (since 1958); post-marketing drug-safety pharmacovigilance.

Defining reference: Thacker & Berkelman (1988). Public health surveillance in the United States. Epidemiologic Reviews.
