Case study (in-depth)

How and why did this happen here, in its real setting?

Study one or a few bounded cases (a company, a project, a policy) in their real context, using many sources of evidence.

How it works

  1. Define the case

    Say what the case is and where it stops: which organisation or group, which period, which evidence. The boundary decides what counts as data.

    “each case should have a pre-defined boundary which clarifies the nature and time period covered by the case study”

    Crowe et al. (2011), p. 5, “Defining the case”

  2. Select the case

    Pick the case for what it can teach: a unique case for its own sake, or typical cases that let you test a theory again in a second or third case.

    “The case is selected not because it is representative of other cases, but because of its uniqueness, which is of genuine interest to the researchers.”

    Crowe et al. (2011), p. 5, “Selecting the case(s)”, on intrinsic case studies

    “to test theory by replicating the findings in a second or even a third case (i.e. replication logic)”

    Crowe et al. (2011), p. 6, “Selecting the case(s)”

  3. Collect many sources

    Gather several kinds of evidence about the same case (documents, interviews, observation, routine numbers) and see whether they agree.

    “The use of multiple sources of data (data triangulation) has been advocated as a way of increasing the internal validity of a study”

    Crowe et al. (2011), p. 6, “Collecting the data”

  4. Within, then across

    Describe and code each case on its own first. Only then set the cases side by side to see what they share and where they differ.

    “In collective case studies, it is helpful to analyse data relating to the individual component cases first, before making comparisons across cases.”

    Crowe et al. (2011), p. 7, “Analysing, interpreting and reporting case studies”

  5. Generalise to theory

    A case speaks to a theory, not to a population. Use theory to explain the case, without bending the case to fit it.

    “allow theoretical (as opposed to statistical) generalisation beyond the particular cases studied”

    Crowe et al. (2011), p. 7, “Analysing, interpreting and reporting case studies”

    “the cases should not be “forced to fit” the particular theoretical framework that is being employed.”

    Crowe et al. (2011), p. 7, same section

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Limited generalizability

    True here, but maybe not anywhere else.

    Run it where the results will be used.

    “Case study research has sometimes been criticised for lacking scientific rigour and providing little basis for generalisation”

    Crowe et al. (2011), p. 7

  • Researcher bias

    You see what you expected to see.

    Write down your own lens, and let a second coder check.

  • Survivorship bias

    You only see the ones that made it back.

    Sample from the whole population, not the convenient part.

A real example

Allison's Essence of Decision (1971) on the Cuban missile crisis; engineering failure investigations.

Defining reference: Crowe et al. (2011). The case study approach. BMC Medical Research Methodology.
