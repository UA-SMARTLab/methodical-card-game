Theory & computation / Method

Prediction model (statistical / ML)

How well can we forecast this outcome for a new individual?

Develop a model that forecasts an outcome from predictors, then test its discrimination and calibration on new data.

How it works

  1. Define outcome

    Say exactly what is predicted, when and how it is measured, and which candidate predictors may go in.

    “Clearly define the outcome that is predicted by the prediction model, including how and when assessed”

    Collins et al. (2015), item 6a

  2. Develop the model

    Combine the predictors, each with its weight, into one formula that gives a risk or probability.

    “Prediction models (also commonly called “prognostic models,” “risk scores,” or “prediction rules”) are tools that combine multiple predictors by assigning relative weights to each predictor to obtain a risk or probability.”

    Collins et al. (2015), introduction

  3. Correct for optimism

    A model always looks better on the data that built it. Resample those data (bootstrap, cross-validation) to estimate how much better.

    “Quantifying the predictive ability of a model on the same data from which the model was developed (often referred to as apparent performance) will tend to give an optimistic estimate of performance, owing to overfitting”

    Collins et al. (2015), introduction

    “Internal validation techniques use only the original study sample and include such methods as bootstrapping or cross-validation.”

    Collins et al. (2015), introduction

  4. Discrimination, calibration

    Report how well the model ranks people (discrimination) and whether its predicted risks match the observed ones (calibration).

    “to quantify any optimism in the predictive performance (for example, calibration and discrimination) of the developed model.”

    Collins et al. (2015), introduction

  5. Validate elsewhere

    Apply the unchanged formula to new people, later or elsewhere, and compare its predictions with what happened.

    “Such external validation requires that for each individual in the new data set, outcome predictions are made using the original model (that is, the published regression formula) and compared with the observed outcomes.”

    Collins et al. (2015), introduction

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Overfitting & leakage

    The model memorized the test.

    Test it on data it has never seen.

    “owing to overfitting (too few outcome events relative to the number of candidate predictors) and the use of predictor selection strategies.”

    Collins et al. (2015), introduction

  • Limited generalizability

    True here, but maybe not anywhere else.

    Run it where the results will be used.

  • Selection bias

    Who ends up in the sample depends on what you're studying.

    Sample from the whole population, not the convenient part.
