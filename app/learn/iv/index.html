Instrumental variable analysis

What is the effect of an exposure when unmeasured confounders cannot be adjusted away?

Find a factor that nudges the exposure but affects the outcome only through it: a natural coin flip.

How it works

  1. Find an instrument

    Look for something that pushes people towards the exposure but has no other route to the outcome, like a coin toss in a trial.

    “An instrument is defined as a variable that predicts the exposure, but conditional on exposure shows no independent association with the outcome. The instrument affects the outcome solely through the effect on exposure.”

    Lousdal (2018), section Three basic assumptions

  2. Relevance

    The instrument must really change the exposure. This one can be checked in the data.

    “The relevance assumption: The instrument Z has a causal effect on X.”

    Lousdal (2018), section Three basic assumptions

  3. Exclusion

    The instrument may reach the outcome only through the exposure. This has to be argued, not tested.

    “The exclusion restriction: Z affects the outcome Y only through X.”

    Lousdal (2018), section Three basic assumptions

  4. No shared causes

    Nothing that causes the outcome may also cause the instrument. A real coin toss guarantees it; a natural one has to be defended.

    “The exchangeability assumption: Z does not share common causes with the outcome Y”

    Lousdal (2018), section Three basic assumptions

  5. Divide

    Divide the instrument's effect on the outcome by its effect on the exposure. A fourth assumption (homogeneity or monotonicity) says whose effect this is.

    “Intuitively, the numerator corresponds to the intention-to-treat effect of the causal effect of assignment on outcome”

    Lousdal (2018), section Three basic assumptions

    “The denominator is a measure of compliance with the assigned exposure.”

    Lousdal (2018), section Three basic assumptions

Why not just compare the exposed?

“When non-compliance increases, the denominator shrinks and inflates the diluted intention-to-treat estimate in order to estimate the causal effect had everyone complied.”

Lousdal (2018), section Three basic assumptions

Each simulated person has a hidden trait U that makes them both more likely to take the exposure X and better off on the outcome Y. A coin Z nudges some people towards X. Compare the naive estimate with the instrument's ratio.

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Confounding

    A hidden third factor drives both exposure and outcome.

    Draw the causal map; adjust only for what belongs.

    “If a suitable instrument can be identified, the causal average effect among compliers may be estimated even in the presence of unmeasured confounding.”

    Lousdal (2018), Background

  • Unvalidated model

    The model rests on assumptions nobody checked.

    Check the code is right, then check the model matches reality.

    “The three basic assumptions allow for identification of an upper and lower bound of the causal effect”

    Lousdal (2018), section Three basic assumptions

  • Low power

    Too small to see the effect, and too noisy to trust a hit.

    Work out how many you need before you start.

    “Often natural variation only gives rise to a weak association between instrument and exposure.”

    Lousdal (2018), section Three basic assumptions

A real example

Angrist & Krueger (1991): quarter of birth, compulsory schooling and earnings.

Defining reference: Angrist et al. (1996). Identification of Causal Effects Using Instrumental Variables. Journal of the American Statistical Association.
