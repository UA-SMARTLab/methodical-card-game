Experimental / Method

Online A/B test

Does this change to a product make users do better?

Randomize users of a digital product to variants. Huge samples detect tiny effects within days.

How it works

  1. Build variants

    Make the change (B) and keep the current version (A). Only that one thing differs.

    “Every major technology company runs online controlled experiments, often called A/B tests, to gather trustworthy data and make data-driven decisions about how to improve their products. All these controlled experiments are randomized.”

    Kohavi et al. (2020), Background

  2. Agree the metric

    Before the test, agree on the one measure (or few) that decides success. Hundreds of other metrics are for understanding, not for picking a winner.

    “Agreement on the Overall Evaluation Criterion (OEC) becomes critically important.”

    Kohavi et al. (2020), Culture change

    “Since the OEC is used to determine success (e.g., shipping a change) and consists of one or a few metrics, there is less concern about multiple hypothesis testing.”

    Kohavi et al. (2020), Culture change

  3. Split at random

    Each user is assigned to A or B by chance as they arrive. Huge numbers make tiny effects detectable.

    “For Bing, Google, and LinkedIn, it is common for each experiment to be exposed to over a million users.”

    Kohavi et al. (2020), Scale and statistical power

    “the ability to detect an effect twice as small, e.g., 0.25%, requires quadrupling the number of users.”

    Kohavi et al. (2020), Scale and statistical power

  4. Check the split

    Before reading results, check that the arms got the share of users they should. A lopsided split means something broke.

    “If the experiment design is for a ratio of one-to-one (equally sized control and treatment) then deviations in the actual ratio of users in an experiment likely indicate a problem.”

    Kohavi et al. (2020), Similarities and dissimilarities with medical RCTs

  5. Ship or drop

    Ship the winner, change or drop the loser, and test the next idea. Most ideas turn out not to help.

    “Winners are shipped, losers are modified (given new data and insights from the experiment) or abandoned.”

    Kohavi et al. (2020), Culture change

    “most ideas are not as good as we believe”

    Kohavi et al. (2020), Culture change

A/A tests: when nothing changed

“Because the null hypothesis is true by design in A/A tests, statistically significant differences for each metric should happen at about 5% when using a p value cutoff of 0.05.”

Kohavi et al. (2020), Similarities and dissimilarities with medical RCTs

In an A/A test both arms see exactly the same product, so any difference is chance. Run a batch of 100 and count the 'significant' ones; then let each test look at more metrics.

Example data, simulated for this exercise. Each arm has 1000 users who convert at 5%; a metric counts as significant when a two-sided z-test gives p < 0.05. Metrics are simulated as independent, which real metrics are not.

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Forking paths & p-hacking

    Try enough analyses and one of them will be 'significant'.

    Lock in the plan before you see the data.

    “It is usually easy to find something that improves (or degrades), but the challenge is to come up with a small set of key metrics, ideally a single OEC, to help make tradeoffs.”

    Kohavi et al. (2020), Culture change

  • Wrong construct

    You measured a proxy, not the real thing.

    Use instruments proven to measure what matters.

    “A good OEC captures the organizational long-term objectives but must be based on metrics that are measurable in short-term experiments.”

    Kohavi et al. (2020), Culture change

  • Contamination & spillover

    The control group catches the intervention.

    Assign whole groups, so nothing leaks between arms.

A real example

Search and e-commerce platforms running thousands of controlled experiments a year.

Defining reference: Kohavi et al. (2007). Practical guide to controlled experiments on the web. Proceedings of the 13th ACM SIGKDD international conference on Knowledge discovery and data mining.
