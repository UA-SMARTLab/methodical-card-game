Descriptive observational / Method

Capture–recapture

How many cases are there in all, counting those no list caught?

Estimate the size of a population you can't count completely from the overlap between two or more independent samples or lists.

How it works

  1. Two lists

    Take two (or more) sources that each record some of the cases, such as hospital registers and a community follow-up. Neither needs to be complete.

    “This method is based on matching two or more independent samples to arrive at an estimate of the total number of diseases/events when both of the lists are potentially incomplete but with a definite overlap.”

    Mony et al. (2015), Introduction

  2. Match

    Find the cases that appear on both lists. With no shared ID number, match on several details at once. In ecology the tag on an animal does this job.

    “In its most classical form an estimate of the size of a species’ population is calculated after capturing, tagging and recapturing animals.”

    Braeye et al. (2016), Introduction

    “If an identifying variable can be recorded on a list at every capture occasion, tagging is unnecessary. The capture-recapture histories become apparent by merging the lists.”

    Braeye et al. (2016), Introduction

  3. Count the overlap

    Sort every case into: list A only, list B only, both. In the Rajasthan study, 12, 20 and 21 early neonatal deaths, 53 in all.

    “Using both methods yielded 53 (N=NAB+a+b=21+12+20) deaths.”

    Mony et al. (2015), Results, estimation of PNMR

  4. Estimate the total

    If the lists are independent, the share of list B that list A also found estimates how much of everything list A found. The paper's form: N = (A+1)(B+1)/(both+1) − 1.

    “The Lincoln-Petersen unbiased formula was used to estimate the true number of perinatal deaths”

    Mony et al. (2015), Methods, analysis

    “The total number of estimated early neonatal deaths worked out to a total of 64 by the CR method (with 11 being missed by both).”

    Mony et al. (2015), Results, estimation of PNMR

  5. Check the assumptions

    The estimate rests on a closed population, reliable matching, independent lists and equal chances of being caught. Lists that catch the same people push the estimate down.

    “The validity of the estimates relies on four key assumptions: (1) the population being studied must be ‘closed’, (2) individuals must be matched reliably, (3) sources should be independent and (4) the chance of being ‘captured’ by a source must be equal.”

    Mony et al. (2015), Introduction

    “As the dependence between sources increases, the bias of the Lincoln-Petersen estimator and the Chapman estimator, will increase”

    Braeye et al. (2016), Introduction

Count what no list saw

“Capture–recapture methods allow for the estimation of an unknown population size by using two or more samples from that population.”

Braeye et al. (2016), Introduction

First the published counts from Rajasthan, then a population whose true size you know, caught by two lists.

The published counts

Early neonatal deaths, Rajasthan (Mony et al. 2015, Results): list A found 33, list B found 41, both found 21. Together they saw 53 different deaths.

Try it on a population you can check

The first part uses the published counts. The population in the second part is example data, made up and drawn with a fixed seed; each dot is a person: amber caught by list A only, blue by list B only, filled by both, empty by neither.

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Selection bias

    Who ends up in the sample depends on what you're studying.

    Draw the causal map; adjust only for what belongs.

  • Measurement error

    The instrument is noisy, biased or drifting.

    Use instruments proven to measure what matters.

A real example

Petersen tagged plaice in the 1890s; epidemiologists now match overlapping disease registers the same way.

Defining reference: International Working Group for Disease Monitoring and Forecasting (1995). Capture-Recapture and Multiple-Record Systems Estimation I: History and Theoretical Development. American Journal of Epidemiology.
