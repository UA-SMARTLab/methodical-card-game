Analytical observational / Method

Mendelian randomization

Does a modifiable exposure cause a disease, judged by genes that shift the exposure?

Use genetic variants as instruments. Genes are shuffled at conception, so carriers mimic a lifelong randomized trial.

How it works

  1. Nature shuffles

    Each child gets a random half of each parent's variants, so the variants are not tied to lifestyle, and the disease cannot change them.

    “Genetic variation is essentially randomly inherited from parents to offspring at conception, and consequently, many factors that confound the association between the exposure and outcome cannot affect the genetic variants.”

    Skrivankova et al. (2021), opening section

    “Similarly, genetic variants are generally not influenced by the outcome and therefore, by reverse causation.”

    Skrivankova et al. (2021), opening section

  2. Pick variants

    Choose variants known, from large genetic studies, to shift the exposure, such as variants that raise LDL cholesterol.

    “MR studies use genetic variants robustly related to modifiable exposures to understand the influence of the exposure on various health, social, and economic outcomes.”

    Skrivankova et al. (2021), opening section

  3. State assumptions

    The variants are instruments, so the instrumental variable assumptions apply: they shift the exposure, share no cause with the outcome, and reach it only through the exposure.

    “relevance (the genetic variants are associated with the exposure of interest); independence (the genetic variants share no unmeasured cause with the outcome); and exclusion restriction (the genetic variants do not affect the outcome except through their potential effect on the exposure of interest)”

    Skrivankova et al. (2021), table 2, Instrumental variable assumptions

  4. Two samples

    Often the variant–exposure and variant–outcome associations come from different studies, combined from published summaries.

    “A type of MR study in which the genetic variant-exposure and genetic variant-outcome associations are estimated in different samples and combined using meta-analysis tools.”

    Skrivankova et al. (2021), table 2, Two sample MR

  5. Estimate

    Relate each variant's effect on the outcome to its effect on the exposure; the slope estimates the exposure's effect.

    “Describe the MR estimator (eg, two stage least squares, Wald ratio) and related statistics.”

    Skrivankova et al. (2021), item 6c

    “Secondly, some examples of natural randomisation processes can be found e.g. Mendelian randomisation, where alleles are allocated at random in offspring.”

    Lousdal (2018), section Three basic assumptions

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Confounding

    A hidden third factor drives both exposure and outcome.

    Draw the causal map; adjust only for what belongs.

    “Concerns about violations of the independence assumption usually focus on confounding by ancestry (or population stratification).”

    Skrivankova et al. (2021), box 2

  • Low power

    Too small to see the effect, and too noisy to trust a hit.

    Work out how many you need before you start.

    “If genetic variants used as instrumental variables are only weakly associated with the exposure of interest, they are said to be “weak instruments,” and then the MR estimates can be biased.”

    Skrivankova et al. (2021), table 2, Weak instrument bias

A real example

LDL-lowering gene variants and coronary heart disease across large genetic consortia.

Defining reference: Davey Smith & Ebrahim (2003). ‘Mendelian randomization’: can genetic epidemiology contribute to understanding environmental determinants of disease?*. International Journal of Epidemiology.
