Genome-wide association study

Which genetic variants are associated with a trait or disease?

Genotype many thousands of people and test each of millions of variants for association with a trait, with a very strict significance threshold.

How it works

  1. Gather people

    Collect DNA and the trait from many thousands of people: cases and controls, or a measured trait such as height.

    “The GWAS is an experimental design used to detect associations between genetic variants and traits in samples from populations.”

    Visscher et al. (2017), section GWAS Rationale and Scientific Basis

  2. Test every variant

    Genotype each person at variants across the whole genome, often millions after imputation, then test each one for association with the trait, one test per variant.

    After Visscher et al. (2017), sections Introduction and GWAS Rationale and Scientific Basis

  3. Strict threshold

    With millions of tests, chance alone gives thousands of p values below 0.05. Only p below 5 × 10⁻⁸ counts as genome-wide significant.

    “where “strong” is defined as statistically significant at the genome-wide p value threshold of 5 × 10−8”

    Visscher et al. (2017), section General Results

  4. Replicate

    Check the hits in independent samples. Well-powered GWAS hits have held up.

    “GWAS associations have proven highly replicable, both within and between populations, under the assumption of adequate sample sizes.”

    Visscher et al. (2017), section General Results

  5. Locus to biology

    A hit marks a region, not a gene or a mechanism. Finding which gene and how is further work.

    “an association between a genetic variant at a genomic locus and a trait is not directly informative with respect to the target gene or the mechanism whereby the variant is associated with phenotypic differences.”

    Visscher et al. (2017), section GWAS Rationale and Scientific Basis

Why 5 × 10⁻⁸?

Test many variants that have no effect at all, and count how many look significant at each threshold.

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Forking paths & p-hacking

    Try enough analyses and one of them will be 'significant'.

    Lock in the plan before you see the data.

  • Confounding

    A hidden third factor drives both exposure and outcome.

    Draw the causal map; adjust only for what belongs.

  • Low power

    Too small to see the effect, and too noisy to trust a hit.

    Work out how many you need before you start.

    “For example, for such rare variants with an effect size of 1 phenotypic standard deviation unit (about 7 cm for height or 5 BMI units), a sample size of more than one million is required”

    Visscher et al. (2017), section GWAS Rationale and Scientific Basis

A real example

WTCCC (2007): 2,000 cases each of seven common diseases against 3,000 shared controls.
