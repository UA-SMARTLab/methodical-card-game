Experimental / Method

Randomized controlled trial

Does the intervention cause the outcome?

Randomly allocate individuals to intervention or control, follow them up, compare outcomes. The benchmark for 'does it work?'

How it works

  1. Enrol

    Screen people against the eligibility criteria. Those who fit and agree are enrolled; the rest are counted, not dropped from view.

    “a clear understanding of these criteria is one of several elements required to judge to whom the results of a trial apply”

    Moher et al. (2010), item 4a

  2. Allocate by chance

    A random sequence, not a person, decides who gets the intervention, and nobody enrolling patients can see the next assignment.

    “With random allocation, each participant has a known probability of receiving each intervention before one is assigned, but the assigned intervention is determined by a chance process and cannot be predicted.”

    Moher et al. (2010), item 8a

    “when properly implemented, it eliminates selection bias, balancing both known and unknown prognostic factors, in the assignment of treatments.”

    Moher et al. (2010), box 1

    “allocation concealment, a critical mechanism that prevents foreknowledge of treatment assignment”

    Moher et al. (2010), item 9

  3. Treat

    One arm gets the intervention, the other the control. Where possible the control looks the same, so nobody knows who got what.

    “The term “blinding” or “masking” refers to withholding information about the assigned interventions from people involved in the trial who may potentially be influenced by this knowledge.”

    Moher et al. (2010), item 11a

  4. Follow up

    Follow both arms for the same time and measure the same outcome. Count everyone who drops out, and why.

    “it may be difficult for readers to discern whether and why some participants did not receive the treatment as allocated, were lost to follow-up, or were excluded from the analysis.”

    Moher et al. (2010), item 13a

    “screening, enrolment, intervention allocation, follow-up, and assessment”

    Eldridge et al. (2016), Fig 5, the phases of the flow diagram

  5. Compare

    Compare the outcome between the arms, analysing everyone in the arm they were allocated to. Because chance made the arms alike, the difference estimates the effect.

    “analyse all participants according to their original group assignment, regardless of what subsequently occurred”

    Moher et al. (2010), item 16, the “intention-to-treat” strategy

Why chance and not a doctor?

“Without randomisation, treatment comparisons may be prejudiced, whether consciously or not, by selection of participants of a particular kind to receive a particular treatment.”

Moher et al. (2010), box 1

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Attrition & missing data

    The people who drop out are not like the people who stay.

    Analyse everyone as randomized, whatever happened next.

  • Expectation effects

    Knowing who got what changes the result.

    The control arm gets an identical-looking dummy.

  • Contamination & spillover

    The control group catches the intervention.

    Assign whole groups, so nothing leaks between arms.

  • Limited generalizability

    True here, but maybe not anywhere else.

    Run it where the results will be used.

    “External validity, also called generalisability or applicability, is the extent to which the results of a study can be generalised to other circumstances.”

    Moher et al. (2010), item 21

  • Low power

    Too small to see the effect, and too noisy to trust a hit.

    Work out how many you need before you start.

A real example

MRC streptomycin trial for tuberculosis (1948), the first modern randomized trial.

“Determination of whether a patient would be treated by streptomycin and bed-rest (S case) or by bed-rest alone (C case) was made by reference to a statistical series based on random sampling numbers drawn up for each sex at each centre by Professor Bradford Hill”

Medical Research Council (1948), p. 770, “The Control Scheme”

“the details of the series were unknown to any of the investigators or to the co-ordinator and were contained in a set of sealed envelopes”

Medical Research Council (1948), p. 770, “The Control Scheme”

“Of the remaining 107 patients 55 had been allocated to the streptomycin group and 52 to the control group.”

Medical Research Council (1948), p. 770

“Four of the 55 S patients (7%) and 14 of the 52 C patients (27%) died before the end of six months.”

Medical Research Council (1948), p. 771, “Results at End of Six Months”

Defining reference: Medical Research Council (1948). Streptomycin Treatment of Pulmonary Tuberculosis: A Medical Research Council Investigation. BMJ.
