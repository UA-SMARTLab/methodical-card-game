Experimental / Method

Field experiment

What happens in the real world when we change one thing?

Manipulate a factor in the real world (plots, islands, villages, markets) with randomized replicates and controls.

How it works

  1. Go outside

    Work where the thing happens (a shore, a field, a village, a market), with its real weather, neighbours and noise.

    “manipulative experiments have experienced a strong increase during the last 40 years, being considered the most rigorous and persuasive tool of hypothesis testing in ecology”

    Aguilera et al. (2018), section 1, introduction

  2. Replicate, at random

    Mark out many plots, the units you will treat. Chance decides which plot gets which treatment.

    “An experimental unit is defined as the smallest entity that can be randomly assigned to a different treatment condition”

    Lazic (2010), Background

  3. Manipulate

    Change one factor on purpose in the treated plots, such as keeping a consumer out with a cage.

    “Herbivory experiments commonly included two to three treatments, in which herbivores are “excluded” (i.e., removed from parcels or plots) and/or “enclosed” in different experimental arenas”

    Aguilera et al. (2018), section 4.3, biotic interactions and experimental design

  4. Control the method

    Leave some plots untouched, and give others the fence without the exclusion, so the cage's own side effects are not mistaken for the effect.

    “followed by “procedural control” (i.e., partial fences or partial antifouling paint”

    Aguilera et al. (2018), section 4.3, biotic interactions and experimental design

  5. Follow and compare

    Return over weeks or months and compare the plots, treatment against both controls.

    “Manipulative field experiments in particular have provided important insight on the complexity and spatial scale of species interactions”

    Aguilera et al. (2018), section 1, introduction

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Contamination & spillover

    The control group catches the intervention.

    Assign whole groups, so nothing leaks between arms.

  • History & secular trends

    Something else changed at the same time.

    A comparison group living through the same period.

  • Low power

    Too small to see the effect, and too noisy to trust a hit.

    Work out how many you need before you start.

  • Limited generalizability

    True here, but maybe not anywhere else.

    Run it where the results will be used.

    “we found that most studies considered a short time (25–200 days), and small spatial extent (~20 m2) in their design”

    Aguilera et al. (2018), section 4.2, spatial–temporal complexity of experimental research

A real example

Paine (1966, 1969): removing Pisaster starfish from a rocky shore collapsed its diversity: the 'keystone species'.

Defining reference: Paine (1966). Food Web Complexity and Species Diversity. The American Naturalist.
