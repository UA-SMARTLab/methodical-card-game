Evidence synthesis / Method

Bibliographic coupling

Which current papers work on the same problem?

Link papers that share references; clusters map current research fronts. Works the day a paper appears, but generic references blur it.

How it works

  1. Gather references

    Take the papers you want to map and list each one's references.

    After Frandsen (2017), section 3.4

  2. Count shared

    For every pair of papers, count the references they have in common. The more they share, the closer their topics.

    “Bibliographic coupling of a directed network indicates the proximity of two vertices based on the number of other vertices to which the two vertices point toward.”

    Frandsen (2017), section 3.4

    “Bibliographic coupling is based on the premise that similarity in referencing patterns can be an indication of topical proximity between source articles”

    Frandsen (2017), section 4.2

  3. Choose a measure

    Use the raw number of shared references, or correlate the two reference lists. Say which, because the map changes with it.

    “The metrics for bibliographic coupling can be calculated as either the number of identical references (Newman, 2010) or the Pearson correlation coefficients of the cited references (Pilkington and Meredith, 2009).”

    Frandsen (2017), section 3.4

  4. Map the fronts

    Draw the papers as a network with the coupling as links, and find the groups. Each group is a research front.

    “Nodes in the network represent citing articles, while edges represent bibliographic coupling, i.e. the number of references shared by two nodes.”

    Frandsen (2017), section 4.2

  5. Works today

    A paper's references are fixed the day it appears, so new papers join the map at once. References everyone cites, such as a textbook, link everything and blur it.

    “A benefit of bibliometric coupling analysis is, that it allows for the inclusion of recent articles.”

    Frandsen (2017), section 4.2

Count the links yourself

Four later papers (bottom) cite four older works (top). Pick two papers and count the references they share: that is bibliographic coupling. Switch to co-citation to count the papers that cite two works together. Then publish a paper today and look for it on both maps.

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Wrong construct

    You measured a proxy, not the real thing.

    Use instruments proven to measure what matters.

  • Selection bias

    Who ends up in the sample depends on what you're studying.

    Search everywhere, including the drawers.

  • Forking paths & p-hacking

    Try enough analyses and one of them will be 'significant'.

    Lock in the plan before you see the data.

A real example

Kessler (1963) grouped physics papers by the references they share; still a standard way to find research fronts.

Defining reference: Kessler (1963). Bibliographic coupling between scientific papers. American Documentation.
