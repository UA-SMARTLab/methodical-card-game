Analytical observational / Method

Case–control study

Were people with the disease more often exposed than people without it?

Start from people with the outcome (cases) and comparable people without it (controls); compare their past exposures.

How it works

  1. Find the cases

    Start from people who already have the disease, ideally all of them or most of them in a defined population.

    “The case sample may be 100% or a large fraction of available cases, while the control sample usually is only a small fraction of the people who do not have the pertinent outcome.”

    Vandenbroucke et al. (2007), box 1

  2. Choose controls

    Sample people without the disease from the same population the cases came from, so they show how common the exposure is there.

    “In general, controls should reflect the population from which the cases arose.”

    Vandenbroucke et al. (2007), item 6(a), explanation

  3. Look back

    Measure past exposure in cases and controls in the same way: records, samples or interviews.

    “In case-control studies, investigators compare exposures between people with a particular disease outcome (cases) and people without that outcome (controls).”

    Vandenbroucke et al. (2007), box 1

  4. Odds ratio

    Divide the odds of exposure among cases by the odds among controls. With suitable sampling it estimates the risk or rate ratio.

    “Results are expressed as the ratio of the odds of exposure among cases and controls. This odds ratio provides an estimate of the risk or rate ratio depending on the sampling of cases and controls”

    Vandenbroucke et al. (2007), box 7

  5. Match with care

    Matching controls to cases on age or sex can help, but it must be kept in the analysis and can bring its own bias.

    “Matching is intuitively appealing, but the complexities involved have led methodologists to advise against routine matching in case-control studies.”

    Vandenbroucke et al. (2007), box 2

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Recall bias

    Cases remember their past differently from controls.

    Use instruments proven to measure what matters.

    “In case-control studies information bias occurs if cases recall past exposures more or less accurately than controls without that disease, or if they are more or less willing to report them”

    Vandenbroucke et al. (2007), box 3

  • Selection bias

    Who ends up in the sample depends on what you're studying.

    Draw the causal map; adjust only for what belongs.

    “Selection bias may be introduced in case-control studies if the probability of including cases or controls is associated with exposure.”

    Vandenbroucke et al. (2007), box 3

  • Confounding

    A hidden third factor drives both exposure and outcome.

    Draw the causal map; adjust only for what belongs.

    “The other factor is called the confounding factor or confounder.”

    Vandenbroucke et al. (2007), box 5

  • Collider bias

    Selecting on a common effect invents a link.

    Draw the causal map; adjust only for what belongs.
