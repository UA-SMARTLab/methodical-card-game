Quasi-experimental / Method

Interrupted time series

Did the trend change when the intervention began, beyond where it was already heading?

Many measurements before and after a dated intervention; test for a change in level or slope beyond the existing trend.

How it works

  1. A long series

    Collect the outcome at regular intervals, for many points before the intervention and many after. Routine data often serve.

    “A time series is a continuous sequence of observations on a population, taken repeatedly (normally at equal intervals) over time.”

    Lopez Bernal et al. (2017), The interrupted time series design

  2. A clear date

    The intervention must start at a known time, such as Italy's ban on smoking in public places from 10 January 2005.

    “ITS requires a clear differentiation of the pre-intervention period and the post-intervention period.”

    Lopez Bernal et al. (2017), Step 1, the intervention

  3. Choose the shape first

    Before looking at the data after the intervention, say what an effect would look like: a drop in level, a change in slope, or both, with or without a lag.

    “It is important that this decision is made a priori based on existing literature and knowledge of the intervention and the mechanism by which it is expected to act on the outcome.”

    Lopez Bernal et al. (2017), Step 2, proposing the impact model

  4. Project the trend

    Extend the trend from before the intervention into the period after. That line is what would have happened without it.

    “In an ITS study, a time series of a particular outcome of interest is used to establish an underlying trend, which is ‘interrupted’ by an intervention at a known point in time.”

    Lopez Bernal et al. (2017), The interrupted time series design

  5. Fit the segments

    Segmented regression fits the trend before, then estimates the jump in level and the change in slope after. In the worked example, admissions for acute coronary events fell by 11%.

    “β2 is the level change following the intervention and β3 indicates the slope change following the intervention”

    Lopez Bernal et al. (2017), Step 4, regression analysis

    “with a decrease of 11% [relative risk (RR) 0.894; 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.864-0.925; P < 0.001)”

    Lopez Bernal et al. (2017), Step 4, regression analysis

  6. Check the threats

    Adjust for seasons, and ask what else changed at the same time. Slow-moving confounders sit in the trend; sudden ones do not.

    “Nevertheless, ITS can be affected by time-varying confounders that change more rapidly. Seasonality can be considered a time-varying confounder”

    Lopez Bernal et al. (2017), Step 5, time-varying confounders

Fit the break

“The hypothetical scenario under which the intervention had not taken place and the trend continues unchanged (that is: the ‘expected’ trend, in the absence of the intervention, given the pre-existing trend) is referred to as the ‘counterfactual’.”

Lopez Bernal et al. (2017), The interrupted time series design

Monthly hospital admissions for acute coronary events in the tutorial's worked example, 2004–2005, as a rate per 100,000; Italy's smoking ban began in January 2005. Compare a plain before-and-after with segmented regression, then move the break to a month when nothing happened.

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • History & secular trends

    Something else changed at the same time.

    A comparison group living through the same period.

  • Regression to the mean

    Extreme values drift back toward average on their own.

    A comparison group living through the same period.

  • Measurement error

    The instrument is noisy, biased or drifting.

    Use instruments proven to measure what matters.

A real example

Hospital admissions for heart attacks before and after a public smoking ban.
