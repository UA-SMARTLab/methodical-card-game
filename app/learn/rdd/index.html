Quasi-experimental / Method

Regression discontinuity

What does the treatment do for people right at the cutoff?

When a rule assigns treatment at a cutoff, compare units just above and just below: nearly identical except for treatment.

How it works

  1. Find the rule

    Look for a treatment given by a rule on a continuous score, such as statins from a 10-year cardiovascular risk of 10%.

    “This variable is then compared to a pre-defined, external, threshold that is used to determine treatment”

    O’Keeffe & Petersen (2025), Overview of the RDD

  2. Check the jump

    The chance of being treated must jump at the cutoff: from 0 to 1 if the rule is always followed (sharp), by less if not (fuzzy).

    “This is known as a “sharp RDD” – where the threshold is a strict separator for the “treated” (above threshold) and “untreated” (below threshold) groups.”

    O’Keeffe & Petersen (2025), Sharp RDD

    “If we do not see a discontinuity at the threshold, we cannot apply the RDD design.”

    O’Keeffe & Petersen (2025), Assumption 1

  3. No sorting

    People must not be able to nudge their score across the line. A pile-up of scores just on one side is a warning.

    “We also assume that an individual cannot manipulate their own assignment variable in an effort to place them above (or below) the threshold and then receive (or not receive) the treatment/intervention.”

    O’Keeffe & Petersen (2025), Assumption 1

  4. Look near the line

    Just either side of the cutoff, people are nearly the same; only the rule separates them. Check that their other characteristics balance.

    “This assumption implies that we consider the threshold to be a quasi-randomising device and that groups either side of the threshold can viewed as similar to randomised groups in a two-group individually randomised RCT.”

    O’Keeffe & Petersen (2025), Assumption 3

  5. Fit each side

    Fit the outcome against the score separately below and above the cutoff, within a window around it.

    “The continuity-based method for RDD uses flexible models for the relationship between the assignment variable and outcome, separately on each side of the threshold.”

    O’Keeffe & Petersen (2025), Continuity-based estimation

  6. Read the jump

    The gap between the two fits at the cutoff is the effect. It holds for people near the cutoff, not for everyone.

    “an effect estimate at the threshold that will be formed using data from individuals with assignment variable values within a small window around the threshold.”

    O’Keeffe & Petersen (2025), Estimand at the threshold

How wide a window?

Each dot is a patient with a 10-year risk score from 0 to 20%; statins are given from 10%, and they lower the outcome. Compare everyone treated with everyone untreated, then fit a line each side near the cutoff and change the window.

Example data, made up for this exercise with a fixed seed (a sharp rule: everyone at or above 10% is treated); the true effect is built in, so you can check each estimate against it.

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Limited generalizability

    True here, but maybe not anywhere else.

    Run it where the results will be used.

    “It is important to remember that the treatment/intervention effect estimate is valid only in a region around the threshold: the RDD does not estimate a global treatment/intervention effect.”

    O’Keeffe & Petersen (2025), Discussion

  • Low power

    Too small to see the effect, and too noisy to trust a hit.

    Work out how many you need before you start.

  • Selection bias

    Who ends up in the sample depends on what you're studying.

    Draw the causal map; adjust only for what belongs.

A real example

Angrist & Lavy (1999): Maimonides' rule capping class size at 40 pupils.

Defining reference: Thistlethwaite & Campbell (1960). Regression-discontinuity analysis: An alternative to the ex post facto experiment. Journal of Educational Psychology.
