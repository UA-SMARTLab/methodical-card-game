Evidence synthesis / Method

Systematic review & meta-analysis

What do all the studies on this question show, taken together?

A pre-specified, exhaustive search and appraisal of all studies on a focused question; pool results where sensible.

How it works

  1. Register a protocol

    Write the question, eligibility criteria and planned analysis down first, and register them, so later choices can be checked against the plan.

    “A review that uses explicit, systematic methods to collate and synthesise findings of studies that address a clearly formulated question”

    Page et al. (2021), Box 1, definition of systematic review

    “Provide registration information for the review, including register name and registration number, or state that the review was not registered.”

    Page et al. (2021), Table 1, item 24a

  2. Search everything

    Search every relevant database and register, plus reference lists and other sources, and record when each was searched.

    “Specify all databases, registers, websites, organisations, reference lists and other sources searched or consulted to identify studies. Specify the date when each source was last searched or consulted.”

    Page et al. (2021), Table 1, item 6

  3. Screen

    Check every record against the criteria, ideally two reviewers working independently, and count what is excluded at each stage.

    “Specify the methods used to decide whether a study met the inclusion criteria of the review, including how many reviewers screened each record and each report retrieved, whether they worked independently”

    Page et al. (2021), Table 1, item 8

  4. Appraise

    Judge each included study's risk of bias with a named tool, domain by domain.

    “Specify the methods used to assess risk of bias in the included studies, including details of the tool(s) used, how many reviewers assessed each study and whether they worked independently”

    Page et al. (2021), Table 1, item 11

  5. Pool

    Where the studies are similar enough, combine their effect estimates, weighting the precise ones more, and measure how much they disagree.

    “A statistical technique used to synthesise results when study effect estimates and their variances are available, yielding a quantitative summary of results”

    Page et al. (2021), Box 1, definition of meta-analysis of effect estimates

    “If meta-analysis was performed, describe the model(s), method(s) to identify the presence and extent of statistical heterogeneity, and software package(s) used.”

    Page et al. (2021), Table 1, item 13d

  6. Rate certainty

    Ask what may be missing, such as unpublished null results, and how sure the body of evidence lets you be.

    “Describe any methods used to assess risk of bias due to missing results in a synthesis (arising from reporting biases).”

    Page et al. (2021), Table 1, item 14

    “Describe any methods used to assess certainty (or confidence) in the body of evidence for an outcome.”

    Page et al. (2021), Table 1, item 15

Pool a forest plot

“Present results of all statistical syntheses conducted. If meta-analysis was done, present for each the summary estimate and its precision (e.g. confidence/credible interval) and measures of statistical heterogeneity.”

Page et al. (2021), Table 1, item 20b

Seven trials of the same treatment report a risk ratio with its 95% interval; below 1 favours the treatment. Pool them with a fixed-effect or a random-effects model, then leave the trials that found nothing 'significant' unpublished and watch the pooled effect move.

Example data, made up for this exercise. Pooling is inverse-variance on the log risk ratio; the random-effects model adds a between-study variance estimated by the DerSimonian–Laird method. Box size shows each trial's weight.

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Publication bias

    The null results are still in the drawer.

    Search everywhere, including the drawers.

  • Apples & oranges

    Pooling apples with oranges.

    Plan in advance how you'll explain why studies differ.

  • Forking paths & p-hacking

    Try enough analyses and one of them will be 'significant'.

    Lock in the plan before you see the data.

A real example

Cochrane reviews; the antenatal-steroids forest plot in the Cochrane logo.
