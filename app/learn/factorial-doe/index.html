Experimental / Method

Factorial experiment (DoE)

What does each of several factors do, and do they work together?

Vary several factors at once in a controlled setting. Estimates each effect and their interactions in few runs.

How it works

  1. Pick factors

    Name the things you can set (factors), and the settings of each (levels), such as on and off.

    “Each intervention and its comparator(s) together comprise a factor.”

    Kahan et al. (2025), box 3, glossary: factor

    “These levels are specific interventions and comparators within a factor.”

    Kahan et al. (2025), box 3, glossary: level within factors

  2. Cross them

    Cross every level of every factor: two factors with two levels give four combinations.

    “All factors and levels are combined so that the design comprises all possible combinations of factor levels, and all participants are eligible to be randomised for each factor.”

    Kahan et al. (2025), box 3, glossary: full factorial design

  3. Randomise

    Chance assigns each unit (a person, a plot, a batch) to one combination, so every unit tests every factor at once.

    “A trial in which two or more interventions are assessed in the same participants.”

    Kahan et al. (2025), box 3, glossary: factorial trial

  4. Main effects

    For factor A, compare everyone with A on against everyone with A off, whatever B was; then do the same for B. Each unit is used twice, which is where the economy comes from.

    “All participants allocated to active A are compared against all those allocated to control A, and similarly for the factor B comparison.”

    Kahan et al. (2025), box 3, glossary: factorial analysis

    “Complete and fractional factorial designs and single-factor designs are generally more economical than conducting individual experiments on each factor.”

    Collins et al. (2009), abstract

  5. Interaction

    If A's effect changes when B is on, the factors interact, and A's average effect describes neither case. Check for it.

    “Occurs when the effect of one intervention depends on whether participants also receive the other intervention.”

    Kahan et al. (2025), box 3, glossary: interaction

    “If treatments interact, then the assumptions underpinning the 2-in-1 trial motivation are no longer valid.”

    Kahan et al. (2025), CONSORT 2010 item 2a, rationale

  6. Fewer runs

    With many factors, run a chosen fraction of the combinations; you give up some interactions to keep the main effects.

    “Some combinations of factors are omitted.”

    Kahan et al. (2025), box 3, glossary: fractional factorial design

    “fractional factorial designs merit serious consideration because of their economy and versatility.”

    Collins et al. (2009), abstract

What the margins estimate

“Trials in which the factorial design has been chosen for efficiency (2-in-1 trials) rely on the assumption that treatments work independently, meaning that the effect of one intervention is assumed to be consistent in the presence or absence of other intervention(s).”

Kahan et al. (2025), CONSORT 2010 item 2a, rationale

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Measurement error

    The instrument is noisy, biased or drifting.

    Use instruments proven to measure what matters.

  • Limited generalizability

    True here, but maybe not anywhere else.

    Run it where the results will be used.

  • Forking paths & p-hacking

    Try enough analyses and one of them will be 'significant'.

    Lock in the plan before you see the data.

    “evaluating multiple interactions increases the risk of false-positive findings, which can affect interpretation.”

    Kahan et al. (2025), box 5

A real example

Fisher's agricultural experiments at Rothamsted; screening designs in process engineering.
