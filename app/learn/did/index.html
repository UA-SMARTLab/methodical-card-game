Quasi-experimental / Method

Difference-in-differences

How much did the outcome change because of the intervention, beyond what changed anyway?

Compare the change over time in a group that got an intervention with the change in a similar group that didn't.

How it works

  1. Two groups

    Find a group that got the intervention and a similar one that did not. Unlike a trial, chance did not decide which was which.

    “The most important distinction is that treatment conditions are randomly assigned across units in an RCT but not in a DID design.”

    Wing et al. (2018), Potential outcomes notation

  2. Two periods

    Measure both groups before and after. Before, neither has the intervention; after, only one does.

    “In the first period, both groups are exposed to the control condition. In the second period, the treatment rolls out in group 2 but not in group 1.”

    Wing et al. (2018), Two groups in two periods

  3. Change in each

    Take each group's change from before to after. The control group's change shows what time alone did.

    “RCT and DID share some characteristics: Both involve a well-defined study population and set of treatment conditions, where it is easy to distinguish between a treatment group and a control group and between pretreatment and post-treatment time periods.”

    Wing et al. (2018), Potential outcomes notation

  4. Subtract

    Subtract the control group's change from the treated group's change. What is left is the estimated effect.

    “DiD estimates the treatment effect by taking the difference between the change in the treated unit and the change in the control units in the periods before and after the intervention.”

    Bouttell et al. (2018), Background and introduction

  5. Check the trends

    The method assumes both groups would have moved in parallel without the intervention. Plot several periods before it and see whether they did.

    “the time series of outcomes in each group should differ by a fixed amount in every period and should exhibit a common set of period-specific changes. Loosely speaking, a graph of the time series should look like a set of parallel lines.”

    Wing et al. (2018), The common trends assumption

    “researchers often plot the mean outcomes by group and time period and then ask whether the lines appear to be approximately parallel”

    Wing et al. (2018), Graphical evidence

Two subtractions, not one

Ten years of an outcome in a treated and a control group; the policy starts in year 6. Compare a plain before-and-after reading with the difference in differences, then let the treated group drift upwards before the policy.

Example data, made up for this exercise with a fixed seed; the true effect is built in, so you can check each estimate against it.

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • History & secular trends

    Something else changed at the same time.

    A comparison group living through the same period.

  • Confounding

    A hidden third factor drives both exposure and outcome.

    Draw the causal map; adjust only for what belongs.

  • Selection bias

    Who ends up in the sample depends on what you're studying.

    Draw the causal map; adjust only for what belongs.
