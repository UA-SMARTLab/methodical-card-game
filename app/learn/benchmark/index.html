Benchmark evaluation

Which method performs best on the same tasks, under the same rules?

Compare methods on shared tasks, datasets and metrics under identical conditions, with baselines, ablations and repeated runs.

How it works

  1. Fix the comparison

    Say what is being compared and why it matters, and whether you are a neutral party or the author of one of the methods.

    “The purpose of a benchmark is to compare, so the comparison that is at the heart of a benchmark must be clearly defined.”

    Sim, Easterbrook & Holt (2003), section 3.2, “Motivating Comparison”

    “The purpose and scope of a benchmark should be clearly defined at the beginning of the study, and will fundamentally guide the design and implementation.”

    Weber et al. (2019), section “Defining the purpose and scope”

  2. Choose the tasks

    Pick datasets and tasks that represent real use, including some where the right answer is known.

    “The tests in the benchmark should be a representative sample of the tasks that the tool or technique is expected to solve in actual practice.”

    Sim, Easterbrook & Holt (2003), section 3.2, “Task Sample”

    “Simulated data have the advantage that a known true signal (or ‘ground truth’) can easily be introduced”

    Weber et al. (2019), section “Selection (or design) of datasets”

  3. Include fairly

    Include the methods by stated criteria, baselines among them, and give each the same care in setting its parameters.

    “certain methods should not be favored over others through more extensive parameter tuning.”

    Weber et al. (2019), section “Parameters and software versions”

  4. Measure

    Score every method with the same metrics, chosen for what users need, and repeat runs where results vary.

    “Performance is not an innate characteristic of the technology, but is the relationship between the technology and how it is used. As such, performance is a measure of fitness for purpose.”

    Sim, Easterbrook & Holt (2003), section 3.2, “Performance Measures”

  5. Renew the benchmark

    Keep changing the benchmark, or methods end up tuned to its particular tests rather than to the problem.

    “Continued evolution of the benchmark is necessary to prevent researchers from making changes to optimise the performance of their contributions on a particular set of tests.”

    Sim, Easterbrook & Holt (2003), section 4.1

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Overfitting & leakage

    The model memorized the test.

    Test it on data it has never seen.

    “In practice, it is necessary to evolve benchmarks to prevent overfitting”

    Sim, Easterbrook & Holt (2003), section 4.1, quoting Tichy

  • Forking paths & p-hacking

    Try enough analyses and one of them will be 'significant'.

    Lock in the plan before you see the data.

  • Wrong construct

    You measured a proxy, not the real thing.

    Use instruments proven to measure what matters.

  • Limited generalizability

    True here, but maybe not anywhere else.

    Run it where the results will be used.

A real example

ImageNet; robot-grasping benchmarks built on the YCB object set.
