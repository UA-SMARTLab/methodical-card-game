Analytical observational / Method

Retrospective cohort

Did people exposed in the past get the outcome more often, judging by existing records?

Reconstruct a cohort and its outcomes from existing records. Fast and cheap, but limited to what someone happened to record.

How it works

  1. Find records

    Look for records that already hold exposure and identity for a whole group, such as employment rolls or health databases.

    “Researchers often use data for purposes for which they were not originally intended, including, for example, official vital statistics that were collected primarily for administrative purposes”

    Vandenbroucke et al. (2007), item 4, explanation

  2. Start in the past

    The cohort's starting line lies in the past; the study idea came after the data were collected.

    “A second usage distinguishes prospective and retrospective cohort studies according to the timing of data collection relative to when the idea for the study was developed”

    Vandenbroucke et al. (2007), item 4, explanation

  3. Trace outcomes

    Follow each person forward in the records, from the starting line to the outcome, death, leaving or the end date.

    “In cohort studies, the investigators follow people over time.”

    Vandenbroucke et al. (2007), box 1

  4. Compare rates

    Compare outcome rates between the exposed and the unexposed, as in any cohort.

    “Investigators commonly make contrasts between individuals who are exposed and not exposed or among groups of individuals with different categories of exposure.”

    Vandenbroucke et al. (2007), box 1

  5. Mind the gaps

    You get only what someone happened to record; a missing confounder cannot be asked about now. Say what the data were first collected for.

    “briefly restating the original aims might help readers understand the context of the research and possible limitations in the data.”

    Vandenbroucke et al. (2007), item 4, explanation

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Confounding

    A hidden third factor drives both exposure and outcome.

    Draw the causal map; adjust only for what belongs.

  • Confounding by indication

    The sickest get the treatment, so the treatment looks harmful.

    Compare new users of one treatment with new users of another.

    “Confounding by indication occurs when physicians or other health professionals select patients for a specific exposure”

    Lousdal (2018), Background

  • Selection bias

    Who ends up in the sample depends on what you're studying.

    Draw the causal map; adjust only for what belongs.

  • Measurement error

    The instrument is noisy, biased or drifting.

    Use instruments proven to measure what matters.

    “Information bias occurs when systematic differences in the completeness or the accuracy of data lead to differential misclassification of individuals regarding exposures or outcomes.”

    Vandenbroucke et al. (2007), box 3

  • Immortal time bias

    The treated get credit for time in which they couldn't have died.

    Everyone's clock starts at the same moment.

    “Eligibility criteria need to be met at that point but not later; study outcomes begin to be counted after that point but not earlier.”

    Hernán & Robins (2016), section Defining time zero

A real example

Occupational cohorts assembled from employment records, e.g. asbestos and shipyard workers.

Defining reference: Euser et al. (2009). Cohort Studies: Prospective versus Retrospective. Nephron Clinical Practice.
