Measurement / Method

Nomological network study

Does the measure behave the way the theory says it should?

From theory, predict which measures yours should track, which it shouldn't, and which groups should differ; then test the whole pattern.

How it works

  1. Write the laws

    Spell out the theory around the construct as a set of linked claims: what it causes, what causes it, what it goes with.

    “Scientifically speaking, to "make clear what something is " means to set forth the laws in which it occurs. We shall refer to the interlocking system of laws which constitute a theory as a nomological network .”

    Cronbach & Meehl (1955), p. 290, “The Nomological Net”

  2. Predict what tracks

    From the network, say in advance which other measures yours should correlate with, and which it should not.

    “If two tests are presumed to measure the same construct, a correlation between them is predicted.”

    Cronbach & Meehl (1955), p. 287, “Correlation matrices and factor analysis”

  3. Predict who differs

    Say which groups should score differently, and in which direction, before you look.

    “If our understanding of a construct leads us to expect two groups to differ on the test, this expectation may be tested directly.”

    Cronbach & Meehl (1955), p. 287, “Group differences”

  4. Test the pattern

    Collect the data and check the whole set of predictions at once. Many varied hits count for more than a few similar ones.

    “Numerous successful predictions dealing with phenotypically diverse "criteria" give greater weight to the claim of construct validity than do fewer predictions, or predictions involving very similar behaviors.”

    Cronbach & Meehl (1955), p. 295

  5. Read the misses

    A failed prediction does not say where the fault lies: the measure, the theory or the other measures. Find out which.

    “If the prediction is not confirmed, any link in the chain may be wrong.”

    Cronbach & Meehl (1955), p. 294

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Measurement error

    The instrument is noisy, biased or drifting.

    Use instruments proven to measure what matters.

  • Forking paths & p-hacking

    Try enough analyses and one of them will be 'significant'.

    Lock in the plan before you see the data.

  • Selection bias

    Who ends up in the sample depends on what you're studying.

    Sample from the whole population, not the convenient part.

A real example

Cronbach & Meehl (1955) coined the idea; Campbell & Fiske's (1959) multitrait–multimethod matrix put it to work.

Defining reference: Cronbach & Meehl (1955). Construct validity in psychological tests. Psychological Bulletin.
