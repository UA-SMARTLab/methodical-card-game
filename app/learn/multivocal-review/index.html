Evidence synthesis / Method

Multivocal literature review

What do research and practice both know about this?

A systematic review that adds grey literature (blogs, white papers, talks) when practice outruns research, and grades each source.

How it works

  1. Decide on grey

    First ask whether the answer lives outside journals: in blogs, white papers, talks and manuals. Decide with explicit criteria, not by habit.

    “A Multivocal Literature Review (MLR) is a form of a Systematic Literature Review (SLR) which includes the grey literature (e.g., blog posts, videos and white papers) in addition to the published (formal) literature”

    Garousi et al. (2019), abstract

    “The decision whether to include the GL in a review study and to conduct an MLR study (instead of a conventional SLR) should be made systematically using a well-defined set of criteria/questions”

    Garousi et al. (2019), Guideline 3, section 4.2

  2. Search both

    Search academic databases for the papers, and the web, specialist sites and people for the grey sources. Follow links both ways.

    “General web search engines, specialized databases and websites, backlinks, and contacting individuals directly are ways to search for grey literature.”

    Garousi et al. (2019), Guideline 7, section 5.1.1

  3. Stop by rule

    A web search never runs dry, so fix in advance when to stop: when nothing new turns up, or after the top N hits.

    “Theoretical saturation, i.e., when no new concepts emerge from the search results anymore”

    Garousi et al. (2019), section 5.1.2, stopping criterion 1

    “Effort bounded, i.e., only include the top N search engine hits”

    Garousi et al. (2019), section 5.1.2, stopping criterion 2

  4. Grade each source

    Grey sources skip peer review, so grade every one: who wrote it, how, whether they sell something, how recent it is, and where it appeared.

    “Apply and adapt the criteria authority of the producer, methodology, objectivity, date, novelty, impact, as well as outlet control (e.g., see Table 7), for study quality assessment of grey literature.”

    Garousi et al. (2019), Guideline 11, section 5.3

  5. Synthesise

    Extract what each source says and code it, mostly qualitatively, keeping a link back to the source. Say how deep the grey evidence goes.

    “Many GL sources are suitable for qualitative coding and synthesis. Some GL sources allow combination of survey results but lack of reporting rigor limits the meta-analysis.”

    Garousi et al. (2019), Guideline 13, section 5.5

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Unvetted sources

    Nobody checked it, and the author may be selling something.

    Grade every grey source before you trust it.

  • Selection bias

    Who ends up in the sample depends on what you're studying.

    Search everywhere, including the drawers.

  • Researcher bias

    You see what you expected to see.

    Write down your own lens, and let a second coder check.

A real example

Garousi, Felderer & Mäntylä (2019) set the guidelines, after an MLR on when to automate software tests.

Defining reference: Garousi, Felderer & Mäntylä (2019). Guidelines for including grey literature and conducting multivocal literature reviews in software engineering. Information and Software Technology.
