Descriptive observational / Method

Case report / case series

What did we see in this patient that nobody has described before?

A detailed account of one or a few unusual cases. Raises a signal or a hypothesis; can't test it.

How it works

  1. Notice

    A clinician meets a patient whose illness, or response to treatment, does not fit what is known. That surprise is the reason to write.

    “They have proved helpful in the identification of adverse and beneficial effects, the recognition of new diseases, unusual forms of common diseases, and the presentation of rare diseases”

    Gagnier et al. (2013), Introduction

  2. Describe

    Write down who the patient is, what they complained of, their history and what the examination found, in enough detail that a reader could recognise the next one.

    “A case report is a detailed narrative that describes, for medical, scientific, or educational purposes, a medical problem experienced by one or several patients”

    Gagnier et al. (2013), Introduction

    “Main symptoms of the patient (his or her chief complaints)”

    Gagnier et al. (2013), CARE checklist item 5b

  3. Timeline

    Put the events in date order: onset, tests, treatments, follow-up. Order matters, because readers will ask what came before what.

    “The timeline should show how the key events of the case unfolded.”

    Gagnier et al. (2013), Results, on checklist item 7

  4. Diagnose and treat

    Say how the diagnosis was reached, which others were considered and ruled out, and what was given, at what dose and for how long.

    “Diagnostic reasoning including other diagnoses considered”

    Gagnier et al. (2013), CARE checklist item 8c

    “Administration of intervention (eg, dosage, strength, duration)”

    Gagnier et al. (2013), CARE checklist item 9a

  5. Follow up

    Report what happened at every follow-up visit, good and bad, including events nobody expected.

    “Adverse and unanticipated events”

    Gagnier et al. (2013), CARE checklist item 10a

  6. Raise a hypothesis

    Discuss what the case might mean and why, then hand it on: one case can suggest a cause, but only a study with a comparison can test it.

    “Case reports may generate hypotheses for future clinical studies”

    Gagnier et al. (2013), Introduction

    “The rationale for conclusions (including assessments of cause and effect)”

    Gagnier et al. (2013), CARE checklist item 11c

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Selection bias

    Who ends up in the sample depends on what you're studying.

    Draw the causal map; adjust only for what belongs.

  • Confounding

    A hidden third factor drives both exposure and outcome.

    Draw the causal map; adjust only for what belongs.

  • Researcher bias

    You see what you expected to see.

    Write down your own lens, and let a second coder check.

A real example

MMWR, June 1981: Pneumocystis pneumonia in five young men in Los Angeles, the first published description of AIDS.
