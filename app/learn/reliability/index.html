Measurement / Method

Reliability & agreement study

Do two measurements of the same thing agree well enough to use either?

Repeat measurements across raters, occasions or devices; quantify agreement (kappa, ICC, Bland–Altman limits).

How it works

  1. Measure twice

    Measure the same people with both methods (or both raters), in random order, over the whole range you will meet in use.

    “Two measurements were made with a Wright peak flow meter and two with a mini Wright meter, in random order.”

    Bland & Altman (1986), p. 307

  2. Plot against equality

    Plot one method against the other with the line on which every point would lie if they agreed perfectly.

    “The first step is to plot the data and draw the line of equality on which all points would lie if the two meters gave exactly the same reading every time”

    Bland & Altman (1986), p. 307

  3. Not correlation

    A high correlation only says the two rise together. One method can read twice the other and still correlate perfectly.

    “Many studies give the product-moment correlation coefficient (r) between the results of the two measurement methods as an indicator of agreement. It is no such thing.”

    Bland & Altman (1986), p. 307

  4. Difference vs mean

    Plot each pair's difference against its mean. The average difference is the bias; the spread of differences sets the limits of agreement.

    “We do not know the true value, and the mean of the two measurements is the best estimate we have.”

    Bland & Altman (1986), p. 308

    “we can summarise the lack of agreement by calculating the bias, estimated by the mean difference”

    Bland & Altman (1986), p. 308

  5. Judge clinically

    Decide whether differences as wide as the limits would matter in practice. That is a clinical judgement, not a statistical one.

    “We shall refer to these as the "limits of agreement".”

    Bland & Altman (1986), p. 308

  6. Check repeatability

    Repeat each method on the same people. A method that disagrees with itself cannot agree well with another.

    “Repeatability is relevant to the study of method comparison because the repeatabilities of two methods of measurement limit the amount of agreement which is possible.”

    Bland & Altman (1986), p. 309

Why correlation fools you

“Correlation depends on the range of the true quantity in the sample. If this is wide, the correlation will be greater than if it is narrow.”

Bland & Altman (1986), p. 308

Two meters measure the same people. The new one reads a little high and scatters around the old one. Widen the range of people measured and watch the correlation climb while the limits of agreement stay where they are.

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Spectrum bias

    It was only tested on the obvious cases.

    Sample from the whole population, not the convenient part.

    “Correlation depends on the range of the true quantity in the sample. If this is wide, the correlation will be greater than if it is narrow.”

    Bland & Altman (1986), p. 308

  • Selection bias

    Who ends up in the sample depends on what you're studying.

    Sample from the whole population, not the convenient part.
