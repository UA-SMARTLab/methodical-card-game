Measurement / Method

Diagnostic accuracy study

How well does this test pick out the people who have the condition?

Apply a new test and a reference standard to consecutive people suspected of the condition; estimate sensitivity and specificity.

How it works

  1. Recruit the suspected

    Take the people in whom the test would really be used: a consecutive series of those suspected of the condition, not a mix of the very sick and the plainly healthy.

    “Whether participants formed a consecutive, random, or convenience series”

    Bossuyt et al. (2015), item 9

  2. Run the new test

    Apply the test under evaluation (the index test) to everyone, with its cut-off for a positive result fixed in advance.

    “The test under evaluation”

    Bossuyt et al. (2015), table 3, the definition of index test

    “Definition of and rationale for test positivity cut-offs or result categories of the index test, distinguishing pre-specified from exploratory”

    Bossuyt et al. (2015), item 12a

  3. Reference standard

    Establish who really has the condition with the best method available, in everyone, not only in those who tested positive.

    “The best available method for establishing the presence or absence of the target condition; a gold standard would be an error-free reference standard”

    Bossuyt et al. (2015), table 3, clinical reference standard

  4. Read blind

    Whoever reads one test must not know the other's result, or what they expect will colour what they see.

    “Whether clinical information and reference standard results were available to the performers or readers of the index test”

    Bossuyt et al. (2015), item 13a

  5. Count the 2 × 2

    Cross the two results in a table: true and false positives, false and true negatives. Sensitivity and specificity follow from its columns.

    “Cross tabulation of the index test results (or their distribution) by the results of the reference standard”

    Bossuyt et al. (2015), item 23

    “Proportion of those with the target condition who test positive with the index test”

    Bossuyt et al. (2015), table 3, the definition of sensitivity

    “Proportion of those without the target condition who test negative with the index test”

    Bossuyt et al. (2015), table 3, the definition of specificity

Move the cut-off

“It is now well established that sensitivity and specificity are not fixed test properties.”

Bossuyt et al. (2015), introduction

Each person has a test value. Above the cut-off the test says positive. Move the cut-off and watch the 2 × 2 table, sensitivity and specificity trade against each other. Then swap the people without the condition: clinic patients with look-alike illnesses, or healthy volunteers.

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Spectrum bias

    It was only tested on the obvious cases.

    Sample from the whole population, not the convenient part.

    “if only healthy controls are included, the estimates of test accuracy may not reflect the performance of the test in clinical applications.”

    Bossuyt et al. (2015), introduction

  • Expectation effects

    Knowing who got what changes the result.

    Nobody knows who got what.

  • Measurement error

    The instrument is noisy, biased or drifting.

    Use instruments proven to measure what matters.
