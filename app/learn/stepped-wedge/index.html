Experimental / Method

Stepped-wedge trial

Does an intervention work, when every group will get it in the end?

All clusters start as controls and switch to the intervention at randomly ordered times until everyone has it.

How it works

  1. All start as control

    Every cluster (a hospital, a school, a region) begins under the usual conditions, and is measured there.

    “The SW-CRT involves randomisation of clusters to different sequences. These sequences dictate the order (or timing) with which each cluster will switch to the intervention condition.”

    Hemming et al. (2018), introduction

  2. Randomise the order

    Chance, not readiness or eagerness, decides which clusters switch first and which wait.

    “A sequence of codes defining the order of implementation of the treatment conditions for each cluster. More than one cluster can be allocated to each sequence.”

    Hemming et al. (2018), table 1, glossary: sequence of treatments

  3. Step across

    At each planned step another group of clusters switches to the intervention and stays there, until all have it.

    “A planned point at which a cluster or group of clusters crosses from control to intervention.”

    Hemming et al. (2018), table 1, glossary: step

  4. Measure each period

    Outcomes are collected in every cluster in every period, before and after its switch.

    “Each cluster contributes data to the analysis from each measurement period.”

    Hemming et al. (2018), introduction, after table 1

  5. Adjust for time

    Intervention periods come later than control periods, so anything that changes over time mixes into the comparison. Compare within periods, and say how time was handled.

    “Observations collected under the control condition are therefore, on average, from an earlier calendar time than observations collected under the intervention condition.”

    Hemming et al. (2018), item 12a

    “A SW-CRT which does not adjust for time is analogous to a simple uncontrolled before and after experiment”

    Hemming et al. (2018), item 17a

Why time must be adjusted for

“Time is a potential confounder because it is associated with both the treatment condition and the outcome.”

Hemming et al. (2018), table 2, imbalance of the design with respect to time

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • History & secular trends

    Something else changed at the same time.

    A comparison group living through the same period.

    “Changes external to the trial may create underlying secular trends.”

    Hemming et al. (2018), item 12a

  • Unit-of-analysis error

    Groups were randomized, but individuals were counted.

    Model people inside groups inside places.

    “An experimental unit is defined as the smallest entity that can be randomly assigned to a different treatment condition”

    Lazic (2010), Background

A real example

The Gambia Hepatitis Intervention Study (1986), which phased in infant vaccination region by region.

Defining reference: Hussey & Hughes (2007). Design and analysis of stepped wedge cluster randomized trials. Contemporary Clinical Trials.
