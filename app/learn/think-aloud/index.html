Design & development / Method

Usability test (think-aloud)

Where and why do real users struggle with this design?

Representative users attempt realistic tasks with a prototype while thinking aloud; observers log where and why they struggle.

How it works

  1. Recruit real users

    Invite people like those who will use the product, not colleagues who built it.

    “whether test users are representative of the actual user population.”

    Nielsen & Landauer (1993), p. 209, factors that change how many problems are found

  2. Give real tasks

    Ask each user to do realistic tasks with the prototype while you watch.

    “user testing is based on bringing real users in and observing them as they interact with the system in order to perform a given set of tasks”

    Nielsen & Landauer (1993), p. 206

  3. Think aloud

    The user says what goes through their mind as they work, without explaining or analysing it.

    “Thinking aloud is the concurrent verbalization of thoughts while performing a task”

    Güss (2018), introduction

    “participants are asked to spontaneously report everything that goes through their minds while doing a task, and they are instructed not to interpret or analyze their thinking.”

    Güss (2018), introduction

  4. Only say “keep talking”

    When the user falls silent, prompt them to keep talking. Do not ask why: that changes how they think.

    “An instruction from a facilitator to “keep talking” while the participant performed a task probably would not disrupt the thought process, though an instruction requiring explanation from the participant, like “Tell me why you did this,” would intervene in the cognitive process”

    Güss (2018), section “Reliability and Validity of Thinking-Aloud Protocols”

  5. Log the problems

    Note each place a user stumbles and what they said there. Each new user finds fewer new problems; stop, fix, and test again.

    “we can expect a Poisson model to describe the finding of usability problems.”

    Nielsen & Landauer (1993), p. 208

How many users are enough?

Each user finds each problem with some chance λ. Nielsen & Landauer's model gives the share of problems found after n users as 1 − (1 − λ)ⁿ. Move the number of users and λ.

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Selection bias

    Who ends up in the sample depends on what you're studying.

    Sample from the whole population, not the convenient part.

  • Expectation effects

    Knowing who got what changes the result.

    Write down your own lens, and let a second coder check.

    “Does the act of thinking aloud interfere with and change a person’ cognitive processes while performing the task?”

    Güss (2018), section “Reliability and Validity of Thinking-Aloud Protocols”

A real example

Nielsen & Landauer (1993): about five users find most usability problems in one round of testing.

Defining reference: Ericsson & Simon (1980). Verbal reports as data. Psychological Review.
