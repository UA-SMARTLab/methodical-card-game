Evidence synthesis / Method

Individual participant data meta-analysis

What do all the trials show, analysed the same way, patient by patient?

Collect and reanalyse each trial's individual participant data, so outcomes, subgroups and analyses are defined the same way everywhere.

How it works

  1. Collect the raw data

    Find every eligible trial, then ask its team for the original record of every participant, not the published summary.

    “large-scale, international, collaborative projects involving the central collection and reanalysis of the original data on each participant, from all of the relevant trials”

    Tierney et al. (2015), Background

    “If nonparticipation is born of a desire to bury unfavourable results, not including such trials could bias a meta-analysis”

    Tierney et al. (2015), Were IPD Obtained from Most Trials?

  2. Fix the analysis

    With so many possible analyses, write down the outcomes and models in a protocol before looking at the data.

    “it is important that the detailed methods of analysis are prespecified in a protocol or analysis plan and are clearly reported upon publication”

    Tierney et al. (2015), Were the Analyses Prespecified in Detail?

  3. Check the data

    Check each trial's data for gaps, impossible values and mismatches with its paper, and resolve them with the trial team.

    “IPD can be checked for missing, invalid, out-of-range, or inconsistent items and for discrepancies with any trial publication”

    Tierney et al. (2015), Was the Integrity of the IPD Checked?

  4. Harmonise

    Define every outcome the same way in every trial and compute the effects yourself, instead of relying on each paper's own analysis.

    “having IPD can allow harmonisation of outcome definitions across trials, analytical assumptions to be checked, and effect measures to be derived directly from the analysis of the collated data”

    Tierney et al. (2015), Were the Methods of Analysis Appropriate?

  5. Keep trials apart

    Compare treatment with control inside each trial, then combine. Throwing everyone into one big table can bias the answer.

    “Thus, a key component of the analysis is to stratify or account for clustering of participants”

    Tierney et al. (2015), Were the Methods of Assessing the Overall Effects of Interventions Appropriate?

  6. Ask for whom

    With every participant's data, test whether the effect differs by age, stage or risk, which summary data cannot show.

    “Rather, the focus should be on estimating the individual participant-level interactions, which is usually only possible with IPD.”

    Tierney et al. (2015), Were the Methods of Assessing If Effects of Interventions Varied by Trial or Participant Characteristics Appropriate?

One big table, or trial by trial?

“combining IPD across trials, as though it comes from a single “mega” trial, could lead to biased comparisons of interventions and overprecise estimates of effect”

Tierney et al. (2015), Were the Methods of Assessing the Overall Effects of Interventions Appropriate?

Two trials of the same treatment. Trial 1 enrolled high-risk patients and gave most of them the treatment; trial 2 enrolled low-risk patients and gave most of them the control. In each trial the treatment lowers the risk. Pool everyone into one big table, then keep the trials apart, and compare.

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Selection bias

    Who ends up in the sample depends on what you're studying.

    Search everywhere, including the drawers.

  • Apples & oranges

    Pooling apples with oranges.

    Plan in advance how you'll explain why studies differ.

  • Publication bias

    The null results are still in the drawer.

    Search everywhere, including the drawers.

A real example

The Early Breast Cancer Trialists' Collaborative Group, pooling patient-level data from adjuvant-therapy trials since 1985.

Defining reference: Riley et al. (2010). Meta-analysis of individual participant data: rationale, conduct, and reporting. BMJ.
