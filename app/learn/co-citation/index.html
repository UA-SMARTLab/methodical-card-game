Evidence synthesis / Method

Co-citation analysis

Which earlier works does this field build on, and in which schools?

Link works (or authors) each time later papers cite them together; clusters map a field's intellectual base. New papers are invisible.

How it works

  1. Collect citations

    Gather a field's papers and their reference lists: which later paper cites which earlier work.

    After Frandsen (2017), section 3.4

  2. Count pairs

    For every pair of earlier works, count how many later papers cite both. That count is their co-citation strength.

    “co-citation, which measures the number of vertices that point toward both vertices i and j.”

    Frandsen (2017), section 3.4

    “In citation analysis, two articles that are similar to other articles typically cite both”

    Frandsen (2017), section 3.4

  3. Cluster

    Link works by their co-citation strength and look for groups: works often cited together pull into a cluster.

    “a high number of co-occurrences among cited references indicates proximity in the ideas of the articles.”

    Frandsen (2017), section 3.4, Figure 1 discussion

  4. Read the base

    Read the works in each cluster to name the idea they share. Together the clusters map the field's intellectual base.

    “Kauffman (1993) and Levinthal (1997) are related as an element in the intellectual structure underpinning the modularity literature.”

    Frandsen (2017), section 3.4, Figure 1 discussion

  5. Mind the lag

    A work joins the map only once later papers cite it, so this year's papers are invisible. Bibliographic coupling sees them at once.

    “A benefit of bibliometric coupling analysis is, that it allows for the inclusion of recent articles.”

    Frandsen (2017), section 4.2

Four older works (top) are cited by four later papers (bottom). Pick two works and count how many papers cite both: that is co-citation. Switch to coupling to count the references two papers share. Then publish a paper today and look for it on both maps.

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Survivorship bias

    You only see the ones that made it back.

    Sample from the whole population, not the convenient part.

  • Wrong construct

    You measured a proxy, not the real thing.

    Use instruments proven to measure what matters.

  • Forking paths & p-hacking

    Try enough analyses and one of them will be 'significant'.

    Lock in the plan before you see the data.
