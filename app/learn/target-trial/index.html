Analytical observational / Method

Target trial emulation

What would a randomized trial have found, using data we already have?

Write the protocol of the trial you wish you could run, then emulate it step by step in observational data.

How it works

  1. Ask as a trial

    Imagine the randomized trial that would answer your question. The observational analysis tries to copy it.

    “Causal inference from large observational databases (big data) can be viewed as an attempt to emulate a randomized experiment—the target experiment or target trial—that would answer the question of interest.”

    Hernán & Robins (2016), Abstract

  2. Write the protocol

    Write that trial's protocol: eligibility, treatment strategies, assignment, follow-up, outcome, causal contrast and analysis plan.

    “Specification of the target trial involves outlining all relevant protocol components of the hypothetical randomized trial (the target trial) that would be conducted to answer the clinical question; this is the causal estimand.”

    Hansford et al. (2025), section The target trial framework

  3. Set time zero

    Make eligibility, the start of the strategy and the start of follow-up fall at the same moment, as they do in a trial.

    “Eligibility criteria need to be met at that point but not later; study outcomes begin to be counted after that point but not earlier.”

    Hernán & Robins (2016), section Defining time zero

  4. Emulate assignment

    Nobody tossed a coin, so adjust for every baseline confounder to make the groups comparable.

    “To emulate the random assignment of strategies at baseline, we need to adjust for all confounding factors required to ensure comparability (exchangeability) of the groups defined by initiation of the treatment strategies”

    Hernán & Robins (2016), section Assignment procedures

  5. Compare

    Estimate the effect the trial would have estimated. It is right only if the emulation, including the adjustment, is right.

    “If the emulation is successful, the analysis of the observational data yields the same effect estimates (except for random variability) as the target trial would have yielded had the latter been conducted.”

    Hernán & Robins (2016), introduction

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Confounding

    A hidden third factor drives both exposure and outcome.

    Draw the causal map; adjust only for what belongs.

    “However, analyses of observational data are often subject to biases, some inadvertently introduced by the investigators (e.g., selection bias due to immortal time) and some that are inherent in the non-randomized nature of the data (e.g., confounding).”

    Hansford et al. (2025), Introduction

  • Confounding by indication

    The sickest get the treatment, so the treatment looks harmful.

    Compare new users of one treatment with new users of another.

  • Measurement error

    The instrument is noisy, biased or drifting.

    Use instruments proven to measure what matters.

A real example

Hernán & Robins' framework; emulated trials of statins and vaccines using health records.
