Design & development / Method

Plan–Do–Study–Act cycles

Does this change improve our service, here, and how should we adapt it?

Iterative small-scale tests of a change in a real service, each measured over time on a run chart and used to plan the next cycle.

How it works

  1. Plan

    Choose one change, say what you predict it will do, and plan who tests it, where and when.

    “A prediction of the outcome of a change is developed in the ‘plan’ stage of a cycle.”

    Taylor et al. (2013), table 2, prediction-based test of change

  2. Do, small

    Try the change on a small scale first: one clinic, one shift, a few patients. Note what goes wrong.

    “As certainty of success of a test of change is not guaranteed, PDSAs start small in scale and build in scale as confidence grows.”

    Taylor et al. (2013), table 2, small-scale testing

  3. Study over time

    Plot the measure at regular intervals before and after the change, on a run chart, and compare it with the prediction.

    “measurement of data over time helps understand natural variation in a system, increase awareness of other factors influencing processes or outcomes, and understand the impact of an intervention.”

    Taylor et al. (2013), section “The origins and theory of PDSA cycles”

    “Methods for understanding variation within the data, including the effects of time as a variable.”

    Ogrinc et al. (2016), item 11

  4. Act

    Adopt the change, adapt it, or drop it, and use what you learned to plan the next cycle.

    “the ‘act’ stage identifies adaptations and next steps to inform a new cycle.”

    Taylor et al. (2013), section “The origins and theory of PDSA cycles”

  5. Cycle again

    Link the cycles: each one builds on the last, at a larger scale as confidence grows.

    “two or more cycles with lessons learned from one cycle linking and informing a subsequent cycle”

    Taylor et al. (2013), section “Iterative cycles”, its definition of a sequence of iterative cycles

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • History & secular trends

    Something else changed at the same time.

    Judge a change against the process's own baseline variation.

    “Approach used to establish whether the observed outcomes were due to the intervention(s).”

    Ogrinc et al. (2016), item 9

  • Regression to the mean

    Extreme values drift back toward average on their own.

    Judge a change against the process's own baseline variation.

  • Researcher bias

    You see what you expected to see.

    Write down your own lens, and let a second coder check.

A real example

The Institute for Healthcare Improvement's Model for Improvement, used in hospitals worldwide.

Defining reference: Taylor et al. (2013). Systematic review of the application of the plan–do–study–act method to improve quality in healthcare. BMJ Quality & Safety.
