Analytical / mathematical theory

What must follow if these assumptions are true?

Derive results from first principles with mathematics: proofs, closed-form solutions, bounds. 'What must be true if…'

How it works

  1. Start from a claim

    Begin with an explanation stated in words: this happens because of that.

    “Progress in science often begins with verbal hypotheses meant to explain why certain biological phenomena exist.”

    Servedio et al. (2014), summary

  2. Write the assumptions

    Turn each ingredient of the claim into a precise mathematical assumption, and say which ones carry the hypothesis.

    “Proof-of-concept models, used in many fields, test the validity of verbal chains of logic by laying out the specific assumptions mathematically.”

    Servedio et al. (2014), section “Proof-of-Concept Models: Testing Verbal Logic in Evolutionary Biology”

  3. Derive

    Work out with proofs or closed-form solutions what the assumptions imply. Mathematics, not intuition, does this step.

    “The results that follow from these assumptions emerge through the principles of mathematics, which reduces the possibility of logical errors at this step of the process.”

    Servedio et al. (2014), section “Proof-of-Concept Models: Testing Verbal Logic in Evolutionary Biology”

  4. Compare with the claim

    Set the derived result beside what the words predicted. Where they differ, the verbal argument had a gap.

    “to test the validity of a verbal model, precise predictions from a mathematical analysis of the assumptions are compared against verbal predictions.”

    Servedio et al. (2014), section “Proof-of-Concept Models: Testing Verbal Logic in Evolutionary Biology”

  5. Check against nature

    The derivation is right by construction; whether it applies is an empirical question about the assumptions.

    “These tests are not “tests of the model”; the model is correct in that its predictions follow mathematically from its assumptions.”

    Servedio et al. (2014), box 2

    “empirical support is essential to ensure that key assumptions of mathematical models are biologically realistic.”

    Servedio et al. (2014), box 1

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Unvalidated model

    The model rests on assumptions nobody checked.

    Check the code is right, then check the model matches reality.

    “if they do not match the intent of the verbal hypothesis, then the mathematical model is not a true test of the verbal one.”

    Servedio et al. (2014), box 1, on critical assumptions

A real example

Carnot's efficiency limit (1824); Nash equilibrium (1950).

Defining reference: May (2004). Uses and Abuses of Mathematics in Biology. Science.
