Evidence synthesis / Method

Umbrella review

What do all the reviews on this condition say, side by side?

A review of systematic reviews: summarizes and grades the evidence across many reviews on a broad topic.

How it works

  1. Frame it wide

    Name the condition and, up front, every treatment and outcome the umbrella will cover.

    “Umbrella reviews (Figure 1) are systematic reviews that consider many treatment comparisons for the management of the same disease or condition.”

    Ioannidis (2009), Simple umbrella reviews

    “Umbrella reviews may be better to perform prospectively, defining upfront the range of interventions and outcomes that are to be addressed.”

    Ioannidis (2009), Simple umbrella reviews

  2. Gather the reviews

    Find the systematic reviews and meta-analyses for each comparison, and keep those of good enough quality.

    “Umbrella reviews are clusters that encompass many reviews.”

    Ioannidis (2009), Simple umbrella reviews

  3. Line them up

    Treat each comparison separately, and set benefits and harms side by side across all of them.

    “Each comparison is considered separately, and meta-analyses are performed as deemed appropriate.”

    Ioannidis (2009), Simple umbrella reviews

    “Compared with a systematic review or meta-analysis limited to 1 treatment comparison or even 1 outcome, an umbrella review can provide a wider picture on many treatments.”

    Ioannidis (2009), Simple umbrella reviews

  4. Mind the seams

    The reviews used different criteria and were updated at different times, and some options were never reviewed. Say so.

    “Patching together pre-existing reviews is limited by different eligibility criteria, evaluation methods and thoroughness of updating information across the merged reviews.”

    Ioannidis (2009), Simple umbrella reviews

    “Finally, qualitative juxtaposition of data from separate meta-analyses is subjective and suboptimal.”

    Ioannidis (2009), Simple umbrella reviews

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Publication bias

    The null results are still in the drawer.

    Search everywhere, including the drawers.

  • Apples & oranges

    Pooling apples with oranges.

    Plan in advance how you'll explain why studies differ.

A real example

Theodoratou et al. (2014): vitamin D and 137 outcomes across meta-analyses and trials.

Defining reference: Ioannidis (2009). Integration of evidence from multiple meta-analyses: a primer on umbrella reviews, treatment networks and multiple treatments meta-analyses. Canadian Medical Association Journal.
