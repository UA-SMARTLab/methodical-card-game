Design science (build & evaluate)

Does the thing we built solve the problem it was built for?

Iteratively design an artifact (device, software, method) that solves a real problem, and evaluate it with users in context.

How it works

  1. Pick a real problem

    Start from a problem people actually have and that has no good solution yet.

    “The objective of research in information systems is to acquire knowledge and understanding that enable the development and implementation of technology-based solutions to heretofore unsolved and important business problems.”

    Hevner et al. (2004), p. 84, guideline 2

  2. Build an artifact

    Design something that addresses it: a device, a piece of software, a method or a model.

    “design-science research requires the creation of an innovative, purposeful artifact (Guideline 1) for a specified problem domain (Guideline 2).”

    Hevner et al. (2004), p. 82

  3. Evaluate in context

    Test it with its users where it will be used, with methods suited to the claim: observation, experiments, analysis or tests.

    “The utility, quality, and efficacy of a design artifact must be rigorously demonstrated via well executed evaluation methods.”

    Hevner et al. (2004), p. 85, guideline 3

  4. Build again

    Use what the evaluation showed to improve the design, and repeat until it works well enough.

    “This build-and-evaluate loop is typically iterated a number of times before the final design artifact is generated”

    Hevner et al. (2004), p. 78

  5. Report to both

    Write it up for those who will build on it or implement it, and for those who will decide whether to adopt it.

    “the results of the design-science research must be communicated effectively (Guideline 7) both to a technical audience (researchers who will extend them and practitioners who will implement them) and to a managerial audience”

    Hevner et al. (2004), p. 82

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Researcher bias

    You see what you expected to see.

    Write down your own lens, and let a second coder check.

  • Limited generalizability

    True here, but maybe not anywhere else.

    Run it where the results will be used.

A real example

Usability-engineered medical devices (IEC 62366); research software built with its users.

Defining reference: Hevner et al. (2004). Design science in information systems research. MIS Quarterly.
