Process tracing

Which explanation of this one case survives the evidence?

Test rival explanations of one case against the evidence each predicts, step by step: hoop tests can rule one out, smoking guns confirm one.

How it works

  1. Pose rival hypotheses

    Write down every explanation that could have produced the outcome, each as a chain of steps, before looking for evidence.

    “the systematic examination of diagnostic evidence selected and analyzed in light of research questions and hypotheses posed by the investigator.”

    Collier (2011), p. 823, the definition

    “Careful, analytically informed specification of hypotheses is essential both in selecting and interpreting pieces of evidence, and in weighing them against one another.”

    Collier (2011), p. 825

  2. Describe each step

    Establish what happened at each moment of the sequence. A chain can only be traced if each link is well described.

    “the descriptive component of process tracing begins not with observing change or sequence, but rather with taking good snapshots at a series of specific moments.”

    Collier (2011), p. 824

  3. Hoop tests

    Look for evidence a hypothesis cannot do without. If it is missing, the hypothesis is out; if present, it merely stays in the running.

    “Hoop tests do not confirm a hypothesis, but they can eliminate it.”

    Collier (2011), p. 826

  4. Smoking-gun tests

    Look for evidence that only one hypothesis would produce. Finding it strongly supports that hypothesis; not finding it clears nobody.

    “this provides a sufficient but not necessary criterion for accepting the causal inference. It can strongly support a given hypothesis, but failure to pass does not reject it.”

    Collier (2011), p. 827

  5. Combine and eliminate

    Single decisive tests are rare. Combine several: rule rivals out with hoop tests and back the survivor with the rest.

    “single tests that accomplish this are rare in social science, but this leverage may be achieved by combining multiple tests, which together support one explanation and eliminate all others.”

    Collier (2011), p. 827, on doubly decisive tests

Who killed John Straker?

“when the investigator has eliminated all plausible alternatives, the remaining scenario must be the correct one.”

Collier (2011), p. 827

Collier teaches process tracing with the Sherlock Holmes story “Silver Blaze”. The trainer John Straker is found dead. Three suspects: the visitor Simpson, Straker himself, and the racehorse. Reveal the clues in any order and watch which suspects survive.

The clues and how each is classified are Collier's (2011, tables 4–6), from a work of fiction. The bar lengths are only illustrative: a straw in the wind multiplies a suspect's weight by 1.5, a passed hoop test by 1.2, a smoking gun by 4, and a failed hoop test removes the suspect. Collier gives no numbers.

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Researcher bias

    You see what you expected to see.

    Write down your own lens, and let a second coder check.

  • Survivorship bias

    You only see the ones that made it back.

    Sample from the whole population, not the convenient part.

A real example

Van Evera's (1997) hoop and smoking-gun tests, now a standard tool in political science.

Defining reference: Collier (2011). Understanding Process Tracing. PS: Political Science & Politics.
