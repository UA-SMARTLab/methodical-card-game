Mixed methods (sequential)

What do the numbers show, and why do people behave that way?

Combine numbers and narratives in sequence: a survey then interviews to explain it, or interviews then a survey to test them.

How it works

  1. Choose the order

    Decide which phase comes first. Numbers first, then interviews to explain them; or interviews first, then a survey to test what they found.

    “In sequential designs, the intent is to have one phase of the mixed methods study build on the other”

    Fetters et al. (2013), “Integration at the Study Design Level”

  2. Numbers first

    In the explanatory order, run and analyse the survey first. Its results, such as an unexpected gap, set the questions for the next phase.

    “In an explanatory sequential design, the researcher first collects and analyzes quantitative data, then the findings inform qualitative data collection and analysis”

    Fetters et al. (2013), “Integration at the Study Design Level” (citation to Ivankova, Creswell and Stick 2006 left out)

  3. Connect the samples

    Choose the interviewees from the survey respondents, by what the survey found: for example, people from both sides of the gap.

    “if the baseline survey data are analyzed, and then the participants sampled based on findings from the analysis, then the design is explanatory sequential.”

    Fetters et al. (2013), “Integration at the Methods Level”, on connecting

  4. Or build a survey

    In the exploratory order, the interviews come first and supply the topics and the very words of the survey items.

    “Items for inclusion in a survey are built upon previously collected qualitative data that generate hypotheses or identify constructs or language used by research participants.”

    Fetters et al. (2013), “Integration at the Methods Level”, on building

  5. Integrate and check fit

    Bring both results together, for instance in one joint table, and say whether they confirm each other, add to each other, or disagree.

    “The “fit” of data integration refers to coherence of the quantitative and qualitative findings.”

    Fetters et al. (2013), “‘Fit’ of Data Integration”

    “Discordance occurs if the qualitative and quantitative findings are inconsistent, incongruous, contradict, conflict, or disagree with each other.”

    Fetters et al. (2013), same section

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Researcher bias

    You see what you expected to see.

    Write down your own lens, and let a second coder check.

  • Selection bias

    Who ends up in the sample depends on what you're studying.

    Sample from the whole population, not the convenient part.

A real example

Explanatory sequential design: a survey finds a gap, follow-up interviews explain it.

Defining reference: Johnson & Onwuegbuzie (2004). Mixed Methods Research: A Research Paradigm Whose Time Has Come. Educational Researcher.
