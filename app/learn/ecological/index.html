Descriptive observational / Method

Ecological study

Do groups with more of the exposure have more of the outcome?

Compare groups (countries, regions, years) using aggregate data. A cheap hypothesis generator; individual-level conclusions are a trap.

How it works

  1. Pick groups

    The units of the study are groups such as countries, regions or years, not the people in them.

    “An ecologic study focuses on the comparison of groups, rather than individuals”

    Morgenstern (1995), abstract

  2. Use group numbers

    Each group gets one number per variable: an average or rate summarised from its members, a feature of its environment, or a property only a group can have, such as a law.

    “Variables in an ecologic analysis may be aggregate measures, environmental measures, or global measures.”

    Morgenstern (1995), abstract

  3. By place or time

    Compare groups side by side in one period, follow one population's rates over time, or do both.

    “whether subjects grouped by place (multiple-group study), by time (time-trend study), or place and time (mixed study)”

    Morgenstern (1995), abstract

  4. Relate the rates

    Plot or regress the groups' outcome rates on their exposure levels. Be clear whether the claim is about groups or about the people in them.

    “The purpose of an ecologic analysis may be to make biologic inferences about effects on individual risks or to make ecologic inferences about effects on group rates.”

    Morgenstern (1995), abstract

  5. Mind the leap

    Group data do not show which people within a group were exposed and also fell ill, so a pattern across groups need not hold for individuals.

    “individual-level data are missing on the joint distribution, of variables within groups”

    Morgenstern (1995), abstract

    “there are many methodologic problems that severely limit causal inference, including ecologic and cross-level bias”

    Morgenstern (1995), abstract

Regions or people?

Five regions, each a circle at its average exposure and average outcome. Look at the regions first, then show the people inside them.

Example data, made up for this exercise; the same seed draws the same people on every visit.

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Ecological fallacy

    What's true of groups needn't be true of their members.

    Model people inside groups inside places.

  • Confounding

    A hidden third factor drives both exposure and outcome.

    Draw the causal map; adjust only for what belongs.

A real example

Messerli (2012): national chocolate consumption vs. Nobel laureates per capita.

Defining reference: Morgenstern (1995). Ecologic Studies in Epidemiology: Concepts, Principles, and Methods. Annual Review of Public Health.
