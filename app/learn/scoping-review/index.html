Evidence synthesis / Method

Scoping review

What research exists on this topic, and where are the gaps?

Systematically map what research exists on a broad topic (concepts, methods, gaps) without pooling effects.

How it works

  1. Ask broadly

    Start from a broad question about what is known, and name the facets that matter: population, interventions, outcomes.

    “As with systematic reviews, the starting point is to identify the research question to be addressed as this guides the way that search strategies are built.”

    Arksey & O'Malley (2005), Framework Stage 1

    “scoping reviews are an ideal tool to determine the scope or coverage of a body of literature on a given topic”

    Munn et al. (2018), Background, Indications for scoping reviews

  2. Search widely

    Search databases, reference lists, grey literature and people, published and unpublished, and refine the search as you learn the field's words.

    “the whole point of scoping the field is to be as comprehensive as possible in identifying primary studies (published and unpublished) and reviews suitable for answering the central research question.”

    Arksey & O'Malley (2005), Framework Stage 2

  3. Select

    Set inclusion criteria, then sift the hits as a team, tightening the criteria as you go.

    “This stage should be considered an iterative process involving searching the literature, refining the search strategy, and reviewing articles for study inclusion.”

    Levac et al. (2010), Table 3, stage #3 Study selection, recommendation 1

  4. Chart

    Extract the same items from every study into a chart: who, where, what was studied, with which methods.

    “describes a technique for synthesising and interpreting qualitative data by sifting, charting and sorting material according to key issues and themes”

    Arksey & O'Malley (2005), Framework Stage 4, on ‘charting’

    “Charting should be considered an iterative process in which researchers continually extract data and update the data-charting form.”

    Levac et al. (2010), Table 3, stage #4 Charting the data, recommendation 1b

  5. Map, not pool

    Report counts and themes across everything found, and point out the gaps. Studies are not graded or pooled, so the map says what exists, not what works.

    “the scoping study seeks to present an overview of all material reviewed”

    Arksey & O'Malley (2005), Framework Stage 5

    “This is because the scoping study does not seek to assess quality of evidence and consequently cannot determine whether particular studies provide robust or generalisable findings.”

    Arksey & O'Malley (2005), Framework Stage 5

  6. Consult

    Show the map to practitioners and other stakeholders: they add sources, test the findings and say which gaps matter.

    “Consultation should be an essential component of scoping study methodology.”

    Levac et al. (2010), Table 3, stage #6 Consultation, recommendation 1

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Publication bias

    The null results are still in the drawer.

    Search everywhere, including the drawers.

  • Researcher bias

    You see what you expected to see.

    Write down your own lens, and let a second coder check.

A real example

PRISMA-ScR guided maps, e.g. of how AI is used in nursing practice.
