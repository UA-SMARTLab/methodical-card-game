Experimental / Method

Cluster randomized trial

Does an intervention delivered to whole groups cause the outcome?

Randomize whole groups (schools, clinics, villages) when the intervention acts at group level or would spill over.

How it works

  1. Define clusters

    The unit you allocate is a whole group: a school, a clinic, a village. Everyone in it gets the same arm.

    “Cluster-randomised trials (CRTs) randomise groups of individuals (“clusters”) to different interventions or sequences of interventions within a trial, as opposed to individuals.”

    Surr et al. (2024), Introduction

  2. Say why

    Cluster when the intervention acts on the whole group, or when splitting a group would let it leak from one arm to the other.

    “CRTs are widely used in settings where interventions are delivered in an attempt to change the culture, environment or general practices and to reduce contamination between arms.”

    Surr et al. (2024), Introduction

    “Rationale for using a cluster design.”

    Hemming et al. (2018), item 2a, the CONSORT cluster extension's wording

  3. Randomise clusters

    Chance allocates the clusters, not the people. Then say how people inside each cluster came into the trial.

    “Specification that allocation was based on clusters rather than individuals and whether allocation concealment (if any) was at the cluster level, the individual participant level, or both.”

    Hemming et al. (2018), item 9, the CONSORT cluster extension's wording

    “Mechanism by which individual participants were included in clusters for the purposes of the trial (such as complete enumeration, random sampling).”

    Hemming et al. (2018), item 10b, the CONSORT cluster extension's wording

  4. Size for likeness

    People in one cluster resemble each other, so each adds less new information. The sample size must allow for it, which usually means more people.

    “The ICC is the between cluster variability divided by the total variability.”

    Thompson et al. (2022), Methods, cluster-level analysis

    “Method of calculation, number of clusters (and whether equal or unequal cluster sizes are assumed), cluster size, a coefficient of intracluster correlation (ICC or k), and an indication of its uncertainty.”

    Hemming et al. (2018), item 7a, the CONSORT cluster extension's wording

  5. Analyse as clusters

    Compare the arms in a way that respects the grouping. Treating every pupil as independent makes the result look surer than it is.

    “How clustering was taken into account.”

    Hemming et al. (2018), item 12a, the CONSORT cluster extension's wording

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Unit-of-analysis error

    Groups were randomized, but individuals were counted.

    Model people inside groups inside places.

    “An experimental unit is defined as the smallest entity that can be randomly assigned to a different treatment condition”

    Lazic (2010), Background

  • Selection bias

    Who ends up in the sample depends on what you're studying.

    No one can see or steer the next assignment.

  • Low power

    Too small to see the effect, and too noisy to trust a hit.

    Work out how many you need before you start.

A real example

School-based deworming and hygiene trials randomized by school.

Defining reference: Campbell et al. (2012). Consort 2010 statement: extension to cluster randomised trials. BMJ.
