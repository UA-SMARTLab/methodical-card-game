Cost-effectiveness analysis

Is the extra health worth the extra cost?

Compare the costs and outcomes of alternatives, often in a decision model, to find the extra cost per unit of benefit, such as per QALY gained.

How it works

  1. Pick the comparison

    Name the intervention, what it is compared with, for whom, and from whose point of view costs are counted.

    “All cost-effectiveness analyses should report 2 reference case analyses: one based on a health care sector perspective and another based on a societal perspective.”

    Sanders et al. (2016), abstract, findings

  2. Count the costs

    Add up the costs of each alternative over the time horizon, bringing future costs and health back to present values.

    “The process of converting future dollars and future health outcomes to their present values.”

    Sanders et al. (2016), glossary, “Discounting”

  3. Count the health

    Measure health gained in quality-adjusted life years: each year weighted by its quality of life, from 0 (as bad as death) to 1 (full health).

    “A measure of health outcome that assigns a weight to each period (ranging from 0 to 1), corresponding to the health-related quality of life during that period”

    Sanders et al. (2016), glossary, “Quality-adjusted life-year”

  4. Divide the differences

    The extra cost divided by the extra health is the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio: the price of one more QALY.

    “The ratio of the difference in costs between 2 alternatives to the difference in effectiveness between the same 2 alternatives.”

    Sanders et al. (2016), glossary, “Incremental cost-effectiveness ratio”

  5. Shake the assumptions

    Vary the uncertain inputs and show how the answer moves. A ratio that flips with one price is not a firm answer.

    “Analysts should be transparent about how they have conducted the analyses, and convey how the results change with alternative assumptions.”

    Sanders et al. (2016), recommendation 4C

Find the price of a QALY

“The ratio of the difference in costs between 2 alternatives to the difference in effectiveness between the same 2 alternatives.”

Sanders et al. (2016), glossary, “Incremental cost-effectiveness ratio”

A new treatment costs more than usual care and gives more health. Move its price and the health gain, then set what a decision maker is willing to pay per QALY, and see whether it passes.

Example data, made up for this exercise: usual care costs 10 000 and gives 5.0 QALYs per patient; the willingness to pay is yours to choose, not any agency's threshold.

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Unvalidated model

    The model rests on assumptions nobody checked.

    Check the code is right, then check the model matches reality.

  • Limited generalizability

    True here, but maybe not anywhere else.

    Run it where the results will be used.

A real example

NICE in England weighs new medicines by their cost per quality-adjusted life year (QALY) gained.
