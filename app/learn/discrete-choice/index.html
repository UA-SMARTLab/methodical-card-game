Measurement / Method

Discrete choice experiment

What do people trade off when they choose, and how much is each feature worth to them?

Respondents choose between hypothetical options whose attributes vary by design; choice models reveal trade-offs and willingness to pay.

How it works

  1. Choose attributes

    Describe the options by a few attributes (waiting time, cost, risk) and give each a handful of levels, found by talking to the people who will choose.

    “DCEs are grounded in theories which assume that (1) alternatives can be described by their attributes, (2) an individual’s valuation depends upon the levels of these attributes, and (3) choices are based on a latent utility function”

    Soekhai et al. (2019), introduction

    “Qualitative research methods continued to be a popular approach for identifying attributes and levels.”

    Soekhai et al. (2019), abstract, results

  2. Design the choice sets

    Combine the levels into pairs of hypothetical options by an experimental design, so each attribute's effect can be told apart from the others.

    “The theoretical foundations have implications for the experimental design (principles to construct alternatives and choice sets) and the probabilistic models used to analyse the choice data”

    Soekhai et al. (2019), introduction

  3. Respondents choose

    Each respondent sees a series of choice sets and picks the option they prefer in each.

    “In a DCE individuals are asked to select their preferred (and/or least preferred) alternative from a set of alternatives.”

    Soekhai et al. (2019), introduction

  4. Model the trade-offs

    A choice model, most often a multinomial or mixed logit, estimates how much each attribute weighs in the choices. The weights are reported as such, or turned into willingness to pay or probabilities.

    “DCEs often presented their outcomes in terms of willingness to pay (WTP), a monetary welfare measure or a utility score”

    Soekhai et al. (2019), section “Outcome Measures”

  5. Check validity

    Because the choices are hypothetical, build in checks, and compare with real choices where you can.

    “DCEs are based on responses to hypothetical choices (stated preferences), so internal and external validity checks provide a crucial opportunity to assess data quality or to compare stated preferences from DCEs with revealed preferences.”

    Soekhai et al. (2019), section “Validity Checks and Qualitative Methods”

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Limited generalizability

    True here, but maybe not anywhere else.

    Run it where the results will be used.

    “DCEs are based on responses to hypothetical choices (stated preferences)”

    Soekhai et al. (2019), section “Validity Checks and Qualitative Methods”

  • Selection bias

    Who ends up in the sample depends on what you're studying.

    Sample from the whole population, not the convenient part.

A real example

McFadden's random-utility models (Nobel prize, 2000), first used to forecast ridership of San Francisco's BART.

Defining reference: de Bekker-Grob, Ryan & Gerard (2010). Discrete choice experiments in health economics: a review of the literature. Health Economics.
