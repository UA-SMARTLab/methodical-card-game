Descriptive observational / Method

Cross-sectional survey

How common is it, right now, and in whom?

Measure exposure and outcome in a sample at one moment. Estimates prevalence; can't show which came first.

How it works

  1. Draw a sample

    Define the population you want to describe and draw a sample from it by a plan written in advance: simple random, or in stages and strata.

    “Most cross-sectional studies use a pre-specified sampling strategy to select participants from a source population.”

    Vandenbroucke et al. (2007), item 12(d)

  2. Measure once

    Measure everyone in the sample at the same time, exposure and outcome together. There is no follow-up.

    “In cross-sectional studies, investigators assess all individuals in a sample at the same point in time, often to examine the prevalence of exposures, risk factors or disease.”

    Vandenbroucke et al. (2007), box 1

  3. Count who answered

    Count how many were invited, eligible and measured. If those who answer differ from those who don't, the estimate drifts away from the population.

    “Those included in a study often differ in relevant ways from the target population to which results are applied. This may result in estimates of prevalence or incidence that do not reflect the experience of the target population.”

    Vandenbroucke et al. (2007), item 13(a)

  4. Estimate prevalence

    Divide the number who have the condition by the number measured. Correct the precision for the sampling design, not just the sample size.

    “They may examine the number of people who have a disease at a particular time (prevalence), or that develop a disease over a defined period (incidence).”

    Vandenbroucke et al. (2007), box 7

  5. Compare groups

    An analytical survey compares prevalence between exposed and unexposed people. It shows an association, but not which came first.

    “Such studies may be analysed like a cohort study by comparing disease prevalence between exposure groups.”

    Vandenbroucke et al. (2007), box 1

Where it breaks, and what protects it

  • Selection bias

    Who ends up in the sample depends on what you're studying.

    Draw the causal map; adjust only for what belongs.

  • Reverse causation

    The outcome is causing the 'cause'.

    Leave a gap between exposure and outcome.

    “A difficulty that can occur in any design but is particularly clear in cross-sectional studies is to establish that an exposure preceded the disease”

    Vandenbroucke et al. (2007), box 1

  • Measurement error

    The instrument is noisy, biased or drifting.

    Use instruments proven to measure what matters.

  • Confounding

    A hidden third factor drives both exposure and outcome.

    Draw the causal map; adjust only for what belongs.

A real example

National health interview surveys estimating smoking prevalence by age and region.
