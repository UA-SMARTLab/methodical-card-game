REFERENCE · SUITS
  • 📊 Describe: What is it? How much, how often, where, and is it changing?
  • 🧭 Explore: What is going on here? How and why do people experience or do it?
  • 🔗 Associate: Do X and Y go together, and does X come first?
  • 🎯 Cause: Does changing X change Y? Does the intervention work?
  • 🔮 Predict: What will happen, and to whom?
  • 📏 Measure: Can we measure it accurately, reliably, and validly?
  • 🧮 Explain: What mechanism or law must hold? What follows from the theory?
  • 🛠️ Build: Can we make something that works, and is the plan feasible?
  • 📚 Synthesize: What does all the existing evidence say?
REFERENCE · CONSTRAINTS 1/2
  • 🚫 Can't assign: You can't (ethically or practically) decide who or what gets the exposure.
  • 🔎 Rare outcome: The outcome is rare: roughly 1 in 1,000 or rarer.
  • ☢️ Rare exposure: Only a small, identifiable group is exposed.
  • ⏳ Long latency: Years or decades pass between cause and effect.
  • ⚡ Brief trigger: The exposure is brief and its effect is immediate.
  • 🗄️ Existing data: Records, registries, logs or archives already exist.
  • 1️⃣ One unit: Only one or a handful of units: one patient, one country, one factory.
REFERENCE · CONSTRAINTS 2/2
  • 👥 Group-level: The exposure acts on whole groups, or would spill over between people.
  • ✂️ Cutoff rule: A threshold on a score decides who gets the exposure.
  • 📅 Rollout: Everyone will get the intervention anyway; only the timing is flexible.
  • 💸 Tight budget: Weeks or months, not years; a small budget.
  • ❓ Uncharted: Little prior theory; you don't yet know which variables matter.
  • 🗂️ Many studies: A substantial body of primary research already exists.

ANSWER · Vanishing butterfliesQ03
🥇 Best Monitoring / surveillance
🥈 Defensible —
  • ⚠️ Traps
  • Cross-sectional survey: One snapshot can't show a trend.
  • Field experiment: Nothing is being manipulated. The question is what is happening, not why.

Trends need standardized counts repeated over many years, the backbone of conservation science. Changes in observers, sites or methods over time are the main enemy.

Watch out for: Measurement error
ANSWER · Tattoo rashQ02
🥇 Best Case report / case series
🥈 Defensible —
  • ⚠️ Traps
  • Randomized controlled trial: Deliberately exposing people to a suspected harm is unethical, and premature.
  • Systematic review & meta-analysis: There is nothing yet to synthesize.

A careful case series is the fastest way to raise a signal that others can test. Its job is to describe and alert. A case-control study is the logical next step, not the first.

Watch out for: Selection bias
ANSWER · City vape countQ01
🥇 Best Cross-sectional survey
🥈 Defensible Monitoring / surveillance
  • ⚠️ Traps
  • Prospective cohort: Follows people for years to measure new cases. The council needs current prevalence next quarter.
  • Case–control study: Sampling people by whether they vape destroys any estimate of how common vaping is.

'What share, right now?' is a prevalence question, answered by one well-sampled snapshot. Put the effort into the sampling frame and weighting, not into follow-up.

Watch out for: Selection bias
ANSWER · Smog atlasQ06
🥇 Best Ecological study
🥈 Defensible Cross-sectional survey
  • ⚠️ Traps
  • Prospective cohort: Years and millions spent on what should be a cheap first look.
  • Randomized controlled trial: You can't assign air pollution.

Comparing aggregate data across countries is the cheapest hypothesis generator there is. Just never conclude that polluted individuals get asthma from it: that's the ecological fallacy.

Watch out for: Ecological fallacy
ANSWER · After approvalQ05
🥇 Best Monitoring / surveillance
🥈 Defensible Retrospective cohort
  • ⚠️ Traps
  • Randomized controlled trial: Seeing a 1-in-50,000 harm would need an impossibly large trial. That is exactly why the approval trials missed it.
  • Cross-sectional survey: Liver injury is an event over time, not a state at one moment.

Post-marketing pharmacovigilance (spontaneous reports plus active monitoring of health records) exists because rare harms are invisible in trials. Signals are then confirmed with analytical studies.

Watch out for: Measurement error
ANSWER · Jupiter censusQ04
🥇 Best Cross-sectional survey
🥈 Defensible Monitoring / surveillance
  • ⚠️ Traps
  • Case report / case series: One spectacular planetary system tells you nothing about the fraction.
  • Analytical / mathematical theory: Formation theory can suggest a number, but this asks for an observed frequency.

ANSWER · Snow shovelQ09
🥇 Best Case-crossover
🥈 Defensible Case–control study
  • ⚠️ Traps
  • Cross-sectional survey: It can't capture the hour before an event.
  • Prospective cohort: You'd need hour-by-hour exertion logs from huge numbers of people to catch a few events.

Brief exposure, acute effect: compare each patient's exertion in the hour before their heart attack with their own usual exertion. Every stable personal confounder cancels out.

Watch out for: Recall bias
ANSWER · Clean-room workersQ08
🥇 Best Retrospective cohort
🥈 Defensible Prospective cohort
  • ⚠️ Traps
  • Case–control study: The exposure is so rare that almost none of the kidney-disease cases in the population will have had it.
  • Ecological study: One factory gives no groups to compare.

Rare exposure: sample on the exposure. Employment records let you assemble the exposed workforce and an unexposed comparison group (e.g. office staff) and trace their outcomes. Comparing with the general public invites the healthy-worker effect.

Watch out for: Selection bias
ANSWER · Rare cancer, common sprayQ07
🥇 Best Case–control study
🥈 Defensible Retrospective cohort
  • ⚠️ Traps
  • Prospective cohort: You'd need to follow millions of pregnancies to collect a handful of cases.
  • Cross-sectional survey: It can't establish that the exposure came before the cancer.

Rare outcome: start from the cases. Draw controls from the population that produced them, and take exposure from records rather than memory, because grieving parents remember differently. A record-linkage cohort works only if registries already capture the pesticide exposure.

ANSWER · Peer tutoringQ12
🥇 Best Cluster randomized trial
🥈 Defensible Stepped-wedge trial
  • ⚠️ Traps
  • Randomized controlled trial: Randomizing pupils within classes lets the method leak to the controls, diluting the effect.

When an intervention spreads between individuals, randomize the group (class or school) instead. Then analyse at the right level, because pupils in the same class aren't independent.

Watch out for: Unit-of-analysis error
ANSWER · New pill vs. oldQ11
🥇 Best Randomized controlled trial
🥈 Defensible Adaptive / platform trial
  • ⚠️ Traps
  • Retrospective cohort: Doctors give new drugs to particular kinds of patients. Confounding by indication makes the comparison unreliable.
  • Case report / case series: Anecdotes can't estimate a comparative effect.

A clean causal question about an intervention you can assign, with genuine uncertainty about which is better: randomize. Conceal allocation, blind if you can, analyse by intention to treat.

Watch out for: Attrition & missing data
ANSWER · Diet and dementiaQ10
🥇 Best Prospective cohort
🥈 Defensible Retrospective cohort

ANSWER · Two inhalersQ18
🥇 Best Crossover trial

ANSWER · Her migraineQ17
🥇 Best N-of-1 trial
🥈 Defensible Crossover trial
  • ⚠️ Traps
  • Randomized controlled trial: A trial's average effect doesn't tell you whether this one patient responds.
  • Case report / case series: An uncontrolled before-and-after story is fooled by regression to the mean: she sought help at her worst.

Frequent attacks and a fast-acting drug: randomize which drug she takes for each attack (or block of attacks), blind it with identical capsules, and repeat. The answer is about her.

Watch out for: Expectation effects
ANSWER · ReunificationQ16

  • Randomized controlled trial: Countries can't be randomized to reunify.

ANSWER · Weld strengthQ20
🥇 Best Factorial experiment (DoE)
🥈 Defensible Computational simulation
  • ⚠️ Traps
  • Retrospective cohort: Past production logs confound settings that operators always changed together.
  • Case report / case series: One great weld proves nothing.

ANSWER · Rat-free islandsQ22
🥇 Best Field experiment
🥈 Defensible Difference-in-differences
  • ⚠️ Traps
  • Cross-sectional survey: Islands that never had rats differ from rat islands in many other ways.
  • Case report / case series: One island's success story can't be separated from that year's weather.

ANSWER · LDL: cause or marker?Q27
🥇 Best Mendelian randomization
🥈 Defensible Randomized controlled trial
  • ⚠️ Traps
  • Cross-sectional survey: It can't separate cause from marker, or tell which came first.
  • Ecological study: Country-level cholesterol averages say nothing about mechanisms in individuals.

People inherit LDL-raising or LDL-lowering variants at conception, essentially at random: a natural lifelong trial. Large genetic consortia make it fast. Pleiotropy (genes acting through other paths) is the threat to check. Trials of LDL-lowering drugs answer a shorter-term version.

Watch out for: Confounding
ANSWER · Does school pay?Q26
🥇 Best Instrumental variable analysis
🥈 Defensible Regression discontinuity
  • ⚠️ Traps
  • Cross-sectional survey: Ability and family background drive both schooling and earnings.

Quarter of birth nudges schooling but (arguably) affects earnings only through schooling, which makes it an instrument. Interrogate that 'only through' assumption hard, and check the instrument isn't weak.

Watch out for: Confounding
ANSWER · Older than the trialsQ25
🥇 Best Target trial emulation
🥈 Defensible Retrospective cohort
  • ⚠️ Traps
  • Cross-sectional survey: It can't capture who started which drug, and when.
  • Case report / case series: There's no comparison.

Write the protocol of the trial you'd like to run (eligibility, treatment strategies, time zero, follow-up) and emulate it in the claims data with new users and an active comparator. This designs out immortal-time and prevalent-user bias; what remains is confounding by indication.

Threats · fronts
THREATconfounding

Confounding

A third factor causes both the exposure and the outcome, faking or hiding an effect.

Coffee drinkers seemed to get more lung cancer, because coffee drinkers smoked more.

Countered by Randomization, Causal diagram (DAG), Matching / propensity scores, Negative controls, Self or sibling comparison, Sensitivity analysis
THREATconfounding-indication

Confounding by indication

The reason someone is treated is itself linked to the outcome, so treatment looks harmful (or helpful) when it isn't.

In routine records, a heart drug looked deadly because doctors reserved it for the sickest patients.

Countered by Randomization, Active comparator, new users, Matching / propensity scores, Sensitivity analysis, Negative controls
THREATselection-bias

Selection bias

Who gets into (or stays in) the sample depends on the exposure, the outcome, or both.

Literary Digest (1936) mailed 10 million ballots, largely to phone and car owners; the 2.4 million returned predicted Landon. Roosevelt won in a landslide.

Countered by Representative sampling, Allocation concealment, Causal diagram (DAG), Comprehensive search, Intention-to-treat, Time-zero alignment
THREATattrition

Attrition & missing data

Participants drop out, or data go missing, for reasons related to the treatment or the outcome.

In a weight-loss trial, the people who weren't losing weight quietly stopped coming. The remaining group looked great.

Countered by Intention-to-treat, Sensitivity analysis
THREATrecall-bias

Recall bias

People with the outcome remember or report past exposures differently from people without it.

Mothers of babies with birth defects search their memory harder for medicines taken in pregnancy.

Countered by Validated, objective measures, Blinding
THREATmeasurement-error

Measurement error

The instrument is noisy, biased, drifting, or used differently across groups or over time.

Self-reported diets underestimate energy intake, and more so in some groups than in others.

Countered by Validated, objective measures, Blinding, Triangulation
THREATreverse-causation

Reverse causation

The outcome (or its early stages) causes the exposure, not the other way round.

Thin older adults die sooner, partly because illness causes weight loss years before death.

Countered by Lag & early-event exclusion, Randomization
THREATregression-mean

Regression to the mean

Units picked for extreme values drift back toward average on re-measurement, with or without any intervention.

Pupils chosen for tutoring after their worst-ever test score improve next time, tutoring or not.

Countered by Concurrent control group, Randomization
THREATexpectation

Expectation effects

Placebo, Hawthorne and observer effects: knowing who got what changes behaviour, symptoms or readings.

Moseley et al. (2002): sham knee arthroscopy relieved pain as much as the real operation.

Countered by Blinding, Placebo or sham control, Reflexivity & double coding, Triangulation
Threats · fronts
THREATcontamination

Contamination & spillover

The control group gets exposed to the intervention, or effects spill over between units, diluting the difference.

Control-group teachers borrowed the new curriculum from their colleagues down the hall.

Countered by Allocate by cluster
THREATimmortal-time

Immortal time bias

The treated group is credited with follow-up time during which, by definition, the outcome couldn't happen.

Oscar winners seemed to live ~4 years longer than nominees, partly because you must survive long enough to win.

Countered by Time-zero alignment
THREATcollider

Collider bias

Selecting or adjusting on a common effect of exposure and outcome creates a spurious association (Berkson's bias).

Among hospital patients, two unrelated diseases look linked, because having either one gets you admitted.

Countered by Causal diagram (DAG), Representative sampling
THREATecological-fallacy

Ecological fallacy

Concluding something about individuals from an association seen only between groups.

Robinson (1950): US states with more immigrants had higher literacy, though immigrants themselves were less often literate.

Countered by Multilevel analysis
THREATunit-of-analysis

Unit-of-analysis error

Groups are randomized or sampled, but individuals are analysed as if independent. False precision follows.

A trial randomizing 10 schools analysed 3,000 pupils as independent and reported impossibly narrow confidence intervals.

Countered by Multilevel analysis
THREATsurvivorship

Survivorship bias

Only the survivors, successes or still-present units are observed; the failures are invisible.

Wald, WWII: armour belongs where returning bombers had no bullet holes, because the planes hit there never came back.

Countered by Representative sampling, Time-zero alignment
THREATpublication-bias

Publication bias

Positive results get published, null results stay in the drawer, and the literature lies by omission.

Turner et al. (2008): almost all positive antidepressant trials were published; most negative ones were not, or were spun.

Countered by Comprehensive search, Preregistration
THREATforking-paths

Forking paths & p-hacking

Flexible choices of outcomes, subgroups and analyses, or peeking at the data, until something is 'significant'.

Simmons et al. (2011) used flexible analyses to 'show' that listening to a Beatles song made people younger.

Countered by Preregistration, Multiplicity correction, Independent replication
THREATlow-power

Low power

Too few units to detect a realistic effect; the 'significant' findings that do emerge are exaggerated.

Button et al. (2013): median statistical power in neuroscience studies was about 21%.

Countered by Sample-size planning, Independent replication
Threats · fronts
THREAToverfitting-leakage

Overfitting & leakage

The model memorizes noise, or sees information from the test data or the future, and fails in the real world.

Chest X-ray AI learned to recognize which hospital (and scanner) an image came from, not the disease (Zech et al., 2018).

Countered by External validation, Preregistration
THREATspectrum-bias

Spectrum bias

A test or rater is evaluated on clear-cut cases and obvious non-cases rather than on the realistic mix of patients or samples.

Tests that look near-perfect on severe cases vs. healthy volunteers falter on the mild, ambiguous patients in real clinics.

Countered by Representative sampling
THREAThistory-trend

History & secular trends

Something else changed at the same time (a trend, the season, another policy) and takes the credit or the blame.

A hospital's hygiene campaign coincided with a nationwide fall in infections and got credit for both.

Countered by Concurrent control group, Pre-trend check & control series
THREATcarryover

Carryover

The effect of an earlier treatment period bleeds into the next one.

Countered by Washout period
THREATresearcher-lens

THREATexternal-validity

The sample, setting or conditions differ from where the results will be used.

THREATheterogeneity

Studies, populations or interventions pooled together differ so much that the average means little.

Pooling trials of different drugs, doses and patients gives a precise average of nothing in particular.

THREATmodel-assumptions

Unvalidated model

A model, derivation or simulation rests on unchecked assumptions, wrong inputs or numerical artifacts.

Countered by Verification & validation, Sensitivity analysis

You measure a proxy or surrogate that doesn't capture what you actually care about.

Countered by Validated, objective measures, Triangulation
